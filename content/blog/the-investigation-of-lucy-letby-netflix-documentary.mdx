**The Investigation of Lucy Letby is a 90-minute Netflix documentary, released on 4 February 2026, about the police inquiry that led to Lucy Letby's conviction for murdering seven babies and attempting to murder seven others at the Countess of Chester Hospital.** It features the detectives, a bereaved mother and footage of Letby's arrests, alongside the lawyer and experts who now say the medical evidence was wrong. It does not change the legal position: her 15 whole-life orders stand, and a review by the Criminal Cases Review Commission (CCRC) is still under way.

This guide explains the case, what the documentary does and doesn't show, where things stand legally as of 25 November 2026, and which podcasts are worth your time. It is written for people who have just watched the film and want the fuller picture, without the noise that surrounds this case online.

A note on how we've written it. Lucy Letby stands convicted, and that is the legal record. There is also a serious, continuing challenge to those convictions by medical experts and her lawyers, which the courts have so far rejected. We try to make clear at every point which is a court finding, which is the prosecution's or the defence's argument, and which is expert opinion. The babies at the centre of this case are protected by court anonymity orders and are referred to here only as the courts refer to them. Facts are drawn from the [Court of Appeal's 2024 judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/crim/2024/748), the [Thirlwall Inquiry report](https://thirlwall.public-inquiry.uk/the-report/), the [CCRC](https://ccrc.gov.uk/news/chairs-statement-on-lucy-letby-application-review) and UK reporting, checked on 25 November 2026.

## Who is Lucy Letby?

Lucy Letby is from Hereford and worked as a nurse on the neonatal unit at the Countess of Chester Hospital. When the hospital described her to police in 2017, as the Thirlwall Inquiry recorded, it said she was highly qualified, worked full time, regularly did overtime and so tended to look after the sicker babies, and that there had been no concerns about her performance.

The Countess of Chester's neonatal unit cared for premature and sick newborns. Between June 2015 and June 2016 the number of deaths and sudden, serious collapses rose sharply. Several consultants grew concerned that Letby was present at many of them, and in 2016 they pressed the hospital to act. How managers responded is the subject of the Thirlwall Inquiry, covered below and in our piece on [what the case revealed about the Countess of Chester](/blog/countess-of-chester-nhs-failures).

## The investigation: Operation Hummingbird

Cheshire Police were brought in after the hospital's chief executive formally requested an investigation on 2 May 2017. No one had seen any baby being harmed. Detectives instead built the case from medical records, the opinions of medical experts, staff rotas and material found at Letby's home. Our guide to [how Lucy Letby was caught](/blog/how-lucy-letby-was-caught) explains how Operation Hummingbird worked.

Letby was first arrested on 3 July 2018, when police recovered handwritten notes from her home. She was arrested twice more before being charged with 22 counts of murder or attempted murder involving 17 babies.

## The prosecution's case

The Court of Appeal summarised the prosecution case as a circumstantial one: that between 2015 and 2016 Letby deliberately harmed babies in her care, intending to kill them. Prosecutors said she did so by injecting air into babies' bloodstreams, forcing air into their stomachs through feeding tubes, overfeeding milk, poisoning two babies with insulin and causing physical trauma. The case rested on:

- **Medical expert evidence.** Paediatrician Dr Dewi Evans was the prosecution's lead expert, and consultant neonatologist Dr Sandie Bohin peer-reviewed his conclusions. Other specialists gave evidence on individual babies.
- **Insulin.** Blood tests on two babies showed readings that the prosecution said could only be explained by someone giving them artificial insulin. The Court of Appeal later said there was "ample evidence" for the jury to find she had poisoned them.
- **Presence.** Shift records were used to show Letby was on duty when the babies on the indictment collapsed or died.
- **Notes and documents.** Notes found at her home included the words "I am evil, I did this". More than 200 confidential handover sheets were found under her bed.
- **Searches.** She had searched for some of the babies' parents on Facebook.

For a closer look at each strand, see our explainers on [the alleged methods](/blog/lucy-letby-methods-explained) and [her notes and digital trail](/blog/lucy-letby-digital-trail).

## The defence's case

Letby gave evidence and denied harming any baby. Her defence argued that the deaths and collapses had natural or medical explanations, and pointed to failings in care on the unit.

One feature of the trial has become central to the later debate: the defence called no expert evidence. According to the Court of Appeal, the defence evidence consisted entirely of Letby's own testimony and that of a hospital plumber, who described drainage problems on the unit. Her current barrister, Mark McDonald, took over the case after the trial.

## The trials and the convictions

The trial began at Manchester Crown Court in October 2022, before Mr Justice Goss, and ran for about ten months. Verdicts were returned between 8 and 16 August 2023. The jury convicted Letby of murdering seven babies and attempting to murder six others (one baby twice), acquitted her on two counts of attempted murder, and could not reach verdicts on six more. On 21 August 2023 she was sentenced, in her absence, to a whole-life order on each count.

She was retried on one of the undecided counts. On 2 July 2024 a new jury convicted her of the attempted murder of Baby K, and she received a 15th whole-life order. Our explainer on [the trial, verdict and sentence](/blog/lucy-letby-trial-verdict-sentence) goes into more detail.

## The appeals and what has happened since

**The Court of Appeal.** On 24 May 2024 the Court of Appeal refused Letby permission to appeal against the 2023 convictions, publishing its reasons on 2 July 2024. It also declined to admit reports from the Canadian neonatologist Dr Shoo Lee as fresh evidence. On 24 October 2024 it refused permission to appeal the Baby K conviction ([judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/crim/2024/1278)).

**The expert panel.** Dr Lee co-wrote a 1989 paper on air embolism in newborns that was relied on at the trial, and he says it was misinterpreted. In February 2025 he presented the conclusions of a panel of 14 medical experts, convened for Letby's defence, who said they did not find any murders. That is their opinion. It has not been tested in court, and prosecutors, police and the families involved maintain the convictions are sound.

**The CCRC.** Letby's lawyers applied to the Criminal Cases Review Commission on [3 February 2025](https://ccrc.gov.uk/news/lucy-letby-application-received-by-criminal-cases-review-commission). In a [statement on 13 February 2026](https://ccrc.gov.uk/news/chairs-statement-on-lucy-letby-application-review), the CCRC said further material had arrived throughout the year, including expert reports in January 2026, and that its review was under way. In March 2026 the CCRC's head of investigations stepped back from the review after concerns about bias were raised, [the Justice Gap reported](https://www.thejusticegap.com/ccrc-head-of-investigations-to-step-down-from-letby-review/). The CCRC can only refer the case back to the Court of Appeal; it cannot decide guilt or innocence. As of 25 November 2026, no decision has been announced.

**No further charges.** On 20 January 2026 the CPS announced it would not bring further charges over allegations involving nine more babies at the Countess of Chester and Liverpool Women's Hospital, saying the evidential test was not met ([RTÉ](https://www.rte.ie/news/uk/2026/0120/1554145-lucy-letby/)). A review requested by families [upheld that decision on 5 August 2026](https://www.cps.gov.uk/national-news/news/decision-not-charge-lucy-letby-further-offences-upheld), which the CPS called final.

**The hospital's former leaders.** Cheshire Police is also investigating possible corporate manslaughter and gross negligence manslaughter at the hospital. On 30 June 2025 three people who were part of its senior leadership team in 2015 and 2016 were arrested on suspicion of gross negligence manslaughter and bailed ([PA, via TheJournal.ie](https://www.thejournal.ie/lucy-letby-hopsital-staff-arrests-6748822-Jul2025/)). No one has been charged, and we have not named them. As of 25 November 2026 we found no report of charging decisions.

Our [latest news page](/blog/lucy-letby-latest-news-2026) and our piece on [the challenge to the convictions](/blog/is-lucy-letby-innocent) cover these developments in more depth.

## The Thirlwall Inquiry: what it found

The Thirlwall Inquiry, chaired by Lady Justice Thirlwall, examined how the hospital and the wider NHS responded to the deaths and collapses. It held public hearings from September 2024 to March 2025 and published its [final report](https://thirlwall.public-inquiry.uk/the-report/) on 15 September 2026.

The report found "a complete failure to protect babies on the neonatal unit", a "prolonged delay by managers in calling the police", and that the doctors' suspicions about Letby were "held in good faith and were based on their clinical judgement", [Nursing Times reported](https://www.nursingtimes.net/midwifery-and-neonatal/thirlwall-inquiry-letby-hospital-failed-completely-in-preventing-baby-deaths-15-09-2026/). It concluded that some deaths and collapses could have been avoided had safeguarding procedures been followed, and made 17 recommendations for the NHS.

What it did not do matters just as much. As the government's [response to the report](https://www.gov.uk/government/speeches/government-response-to-the-thirlwall-inquiry) put it, it was not Lady Justice Thirlwall's role to look at the convictions, the legal process or the court evidence. The inquiry proceeded on the basis of the jury's verdicts. It neither confirms nor undermines them, and the CCRC review is a separate process.

## The role of the medical evidence

This is the heart of the continuing debate, and it helps to separate three things.

1. **What the jury decided.** Twelve jurors, and later a second jury, heard months of evidence and were sure Letby deliberately harmed the babies on the counts where they convicted. That is the legal finding.
2. **What the Court of Appeal decided.** The appeal judges looked at the challenges to the expert evidence available in 2024, including Dr Lee's, and found no arguable ground of appeal. They stressed that the medical evidence had to be considered alongside all the other circumstantial evidence, not in isolation.
3. **What critics now argue.** Dr Lee's panel, Letby's lawyers and some statisticians and doctors argue that the babies' deaths and collapses can be explained by natural causes and poor care, and that the prosecution's experts were wrong. These are expert opinions submitted to the CCRC. No court has accepted them.

If you want to understand the expert-evidence debate rather than just its conclusions, The Slow Newscast episode recommended below is the best single listen.

## Netflix's The Investigation of Lucy Letby: what the documentary covers

[*The Investigation of Lucy Letby*](https://www.netflix.com/title/81719673) is a feature-length documentary, running about an hour and a half, directed by Dominic Sivyer and made by ITN Productions for Netflix. It was released worldwide on 4 February 2026. [Netflix describes it](https://media.netflix.com/en/only-on-netflix/81719673) as giving access to "the detectives who built the case, the families seeking answers, the hospital consultants who initially raised the alarm, and lawyers and medical experts who question the evidence".

Who appears, according to reporting by [Time](https://time.com/7363946/lucy-letby-netflix-documentary/) and [Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/lucy-letby-documentary-netflix-footage-trailer-release-date-11430592):

- **The police.** Detectives from Cheshire Police, including Detective Superintendent Simon Blackwell, who says that "objectively, it couldn't have been anyone else".
- **A consultant who raised concerns.** Retired consultant paediatrician Dr John Gibbs.
- **A bereaved mother.** Her identity is protected: the film uses AI to anonymise her face, and her baby's name is changed. She, like the investigators, maintains that Letby is guilty.
- **The challenge to the convictions.** Letby's barrister, Mark McDonald, and Dr Shoo Lee, who says in the film: "If my paper was misinterpreted, we have a big problem on our hands."

Lucy Letby was not interviewed for the film. She appears only in police footage.

### What is new in the documentary?

- **The arrest footage.** The most widely discussed material is body-worn camera footage, provided to the filmmakers by Cheshire Police, of Letby being arrested on three separate occasions, [Slate reported](https://slate.com/culture/2026/02/netflix-lucy-letby-uk-nurse-baby-murder-documentary-investigation.html). The film opens with officers entering her parents' home. Material from her police interviews is also included.
- **The detectives' own account.** It is the first time the investigating officers have told the story of Operation Hummingbird at length on screen.
- **A family member's perspective.** The bereaved mother's interview brings in a voice that has rarely been heard outside the courtroom.

What it does not contain is new evidence about what happened to the babies. Letby's parents, John and Susan Letby, said before release that the footage of their Hereford home was "a complete invasion of privacy" and that they had no plans to watch the film, [according to the Sunday Times](https://www.timesandstar.co.uk/news/national/uk-today/25815675.lucy-letbys-parents-speak-netflix-documentary/).

### Is The Investigation of Lucy Letby worth watching?

Yes, with clear expectations. Its real value is in putting the people who built the case and the people who now challenge it side by side, so you can hear the investigators explain why they are sure, and hear Dr Lee and Mark McDonald explain why they are not. For many viewers it will also be the first time they have heard from a bereaved parent, and that is a reminder of who this case is about.

Its limits are just as clear. Ninety minutes cannot do justice to a ten-month trial and 15 counts, and the film adds no new evidence. Critics noticed. In The Observer, Wendy Ide wrote that "with no new revelations pertaining to the case, the main talking point from this documentary looks likely to be the footage of the arrests"; The Times gave it three stars; and Slate called it "an interesting and uncomfortable document of whose privacy is deemed important in a case like this" ([reviews collected by Rotten Tomatoes](https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/the_investigation_of_lucy_letby)). The use of AI to disguise a contributor's face has also divided viewers.

Above all, it was released in February 2026, so it predates both the Thirlwall report and the CPS's final decision on further charges. Watch it as one account of the investigation, not as a verdict on the case, and pair it with the reporting and podcasts below.

## What readers should understand about the continuing debate

**The convictions stand.** A jury convicted Letby, a second jury convicted her on a retrial count, and the Court of Appeal twice refused permission to appeal. Only the Court of Appeal can quash those convictions, and it can only reconsider them if the CCRC refers the case back.

**The challenge is serious, but it has not succeeded.** Dr Lee's panel, Letby's barrister, a number of scientists and some politicians argue the medical evidence was wrong. Those arguments are now with the CCRC. A body of expert opinion, however eminent, is not a court finding, and the prosecution's experts and the families stand by the verdicts.

**The inquiry and the documentary answer different questions.** The Thirlwall report examined how the hospital responded and found profound failings. It did not ask whether Letby is guilty. The Netflix film tells the story of the investigation and the doubts raised since, but it cannot change the legal position either.

**The families are at the centre of this.** Whatever view anyone takes, the people most affected are the parents of the babies who died or were harmed, some of whom are now living through renewed public argument about their children's deaths. We'd encourage anyone discussing the case to keep that in mind, and our piece on [the ethics of true crime podcasting](/blog/ethics-of-true-crime-podcasting) explores why it matters.

## The bottom line

*The Investigation of Lucy Letby* is a useful starting point: it puts the detectives, a bereaved mother and the experts who doubt the verdicts in one film, and shows footage never seen before. It is not the whole story. Since its release, the CPS has finally ruled out further charges, the Thirlwall Inquiry has reported, and the CCRC review continues. For the full picture, read the inquiry's summary, listen to the reporting above, and treat any single documentary, including this one, as one account among several.
