Some of the best true crime is not really about the crime. It is about the work: the pathologist who has to decide what a body can tell her, the detective who spends twenty years on one box of evidence, the hospital administrator who had the warning signs and filed them. These stories reward close attention, and they tend to be told by people who were there.

This guide gathers books and podcasts for listeners who want that angle. They are about forensics, cold case work and medical crime, and all of them lean on professional expertise rather than speculation. Some descriptions in this area are necessarily frank, and we say where.

## Who this guide is for

This is for listeners who have worked through the headline cases and want to understand the machinery behind them: how a cause of death is decided, how a cold case is reopened, how a hospital can miss what is in front of it. It is not a guide to the most dramatic stories. The works here are chosen because they explain a profession or a process, and they are generally less sensational than a typical serial killer documentary. If you want the dramatic end of the genre, our [serial killer books and audiobooks](/blog/best-serial-killer-books-and-audiobooks) guide is a better fit.

## The medical examiner's view

### Working Stiff — Judy Melinek and T. J. Mitchell

This is a forensic pathologist's account of her first two years as a New York City medical examiner, including the aftermath of the attacks of 11 September 2001. It is less about solving crimes than about what the job asks of the people who do it: deciding the cause and manner of a death, dealing with grieving families, and coping with the volume.

It is the most frank book on this list. The writing treats bodies and injuries plainly because that is the work, and some readers will find it heavy. If you want to understand what a forensic pathologist actually does, though, it is a good place to start.

**Pair it with** [Anatomy of Murder](/podcasts/anatomy-of-murder), a weekly podcast of about 50 minutes per episode and 250 or more cases covered, which we list as good if niche interests you and which is aimed at listeners who want professional expertise and insider perspective rather than amateur commentary.

## The hospital that did not look

### The Good Nurse — Charles Graeber

The book covers the US case of Charles Cullen, a nurse who admitted killing patients in hospitals in New Jersey and Pennsylvania. Its real subject is the institutional failure around him: how a pattern was noticed, reported and moved on rather than stopped.

That is the same theme as much of the best medical crime reporting, and it is why we include it here. It is a book about a system as much as a person. For the podcast equivalent, [Dr. Death](/podcasts/dr-death) tells the story of a surgeon and the institutions that allowed him to keep operating. We rate it a **Must listen** with a binge factor of 8, and it runs for six to eight episodes of around 30 to 45 minutes each season. Our [Christopher Duntsch case page](/cases/christopher-duntsch) gives the background.

For more shows on the same theme, see our [best medical crime podcasts](/blog/best-medical-crime-podcasts) and the list of [podcasts like Dr. Death](/podcasts-like/dr-death).

## The cold case investigator's view

### Unmasked — Paul Holes

Holes is a cold case investigator, and his memoir covers his career, including his work on the Golden State Killer case. It is the most personal book here, and the one that shows most clearly what the work costs the person doing it. The case that made his name is covered in our [Golden State Killer guide](/blog/golden-state-killer-podcasts-and-books).

**Pair it with** [The Cold Case Investigator](/podcasts/the-cold-case-investigator), a podcast in which a former cold case detective shares investigation techniques and reviews famous cold cases, which we list as good if US true crime interests you. ## The detectives who meet to review the unsolved

### The Murder Room — Michael Capuzzo

The Vidocq Society is a Philadelphia group of detectives and forensic specialists who meet to review cold cases. Capuzzo's book is about them, and it is a very different kind of true crime book from a case narrative: it is about the *method*, with the group as the subject.

It is an easy book to dip into, because it is built around a series of cases rather than one long thread. It suits listeners who like the idea of a team of experts looking at old evidence with fresh eyes.

## A podcast for the DNA question

[A Killing on the Cape](/podcasts/a-killing-on-the-cape) deserves a mention in this company. It is a **Must listen** on our site, with a binge factor of 7, and it examines forensic DNA evidence and a New England murder, raising the question of whether mass DNA collection to solve a single case is proportionate. It is a good example of the genre taking a scientific question seriously.

If that angle interests you, our guide to [genetic genealogy and cold cases](/blog/genetic-genealogy-cold-cases) explains how DNA databases have changed investigations.

## Voices from the FBI

Two podcasts on the site are built around retired federal investigators. [FBI Retired Case File Review](/podcasts/fbi-retired-case-file-review), hosted by Jerri Williams, interviews fellow FBI veterans about their most memorable cases, runs to more than 100 episodes of 40 to 60 minutes and is a **Must listen** on our scale, with a binge factor of 8. [The Consult: Real FBI Profilers](/podcasts/the-consult-real-fbi-profilers) has retired profilers discuss the behaviour behind crimes they worked on, over 163 episodes of around 55 minutes. We have not rated that one, and as with *Mindhunter*, it is worth remembering that profiling is a contested discipline, so treat what you hear as experienced opinion rather than established science.

## Reading the evidence carefully

Forensic books and podcasts have a particular responsibility, because they ask the audience to trust expertise. Two cautions are worth having in mind.

**Forensic science has limits.** Some techniques that were once presented as certain have since been questioned, and wrongful convictions have turned on evidence that did not hold up. Our guide to [wrongful conviction podcasts](/blog/wrongful-conviction-podcasts) covers that side of the field.

**Expertise is not the same as accuracy.** A confident professional voice is persuasive, and it can be wrong. The better works in this area say so, and the best ones show their working.
