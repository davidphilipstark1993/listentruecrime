Audible is the first name most people think of for audiobooks, and it is also a paid subscription that renews until you cancel it. For a true crime fan who already has hundreds of free podcasts to choose from, that raises a fair question: is it worth paying for?

The honest answer is that it depends on how you listen. This guide sets out who is likely to get value, who probably will not, and what to try first. It is written with a conflict of interest in plain sight, which we explain next.

## What Audible is, and what it isn't

Audible is Amazon's audiobook service. Membership is paid and renews until cancelled, and the terms are set by Audible, not by us. It carries a large catalogue of true crime audiobooks, and it is where many people first try the format.

It is not the only way to listen to an audiobook. You can buy individual audiobooks without a membership, you can borrow them from some libraries, and some other subscription services now include a limited allowance of audiobooks alongside music or podcasts. Check what you already pay for before adding something new.

We have deliberately not quoted prices, credit allowances or trial lengths here. They change often and vary by offer, and a number we print today could be wrong by the time you read it.

## Who is it likely to be worth it for

### You finish books, and you want specific ones

If there are particular titles you have wanted to read and would buy anyway, a membership can make sense. True crime books are long and tend to be listened to over weeks, which suits an audiobook habit.

### You like the same story in more than one format

Plenty of people hear a podcast series and immediately want the book it came from. Serialised podcasts such as [Serial](/podcasts/serial), [In the Dark](/podcasts/in-the-dark) and [The Dropout](/podcasts/the-dropout) are a few of the shows that send listeners looking for more depth. If that is you, audiobooks are the natural extension. Our guide to the [best true crime audiobooks on Audible](/blog/best-true-crime-audiobooks-on-audible) matches each book to a podcast on the same case.

### You have long, repetitive stretches of time

Commutes, long drives, gym sessions and housework are where audiobooks earn their place. If you already fill those with podcasts and run out of episodes of the shows you like, audiobooks are a way to refill the queue.

## Who it probably isn't for

### You rarely finish a book

An audiobook is a commitment of many hours, and if you tend to drift away from long stories, a subscription can sit unused. If you are not sure, try a single audiobook first rather than starting with a membership.

### You are happy with podcasts

True crime podcasts are free, and the best are as good as many books. Our guides to the [best true crime podcasts](/best-true-crime-podcasts) and the [best true crime podcasts for beginners](/blog/best-true-crime-podcasts-for-beginners) alone will keep most people busy for months. If you have not run out of things to listen to, you may not need to pay for more.

### You can borrow instead

Many UK public libraries offer audiobooks through lending apps, and borrowing costs nothing beyond a library card. The catalogue is smaller than a commercial service, and popular titles can have a waiting list, but it is worth checking first. We have not checked any particular library's catalogue, so look at what yours offers.

## Audiobook or podcast: what actually differs

The two formats feel similar in your headphones but are built differently, and that affects which suits you.

**A podcast is produced for audio.** It is scripted or edited for the ear, released in episodes and sometimes updated as a case develops. Serialised shows are designed to be binged, and many include interviews, archive audio and sound design.

**An audiobook is a book read aloud.** It is fixed at publication, usually read by one narrator, and it carries the research of a book: more detail, more names, and a single author's argument sustained over many hours. What you lose in audio is the apparatus. Footnotes, maps, photographs and indexes do not translate well, so if a book relies on them, a print or ebook copy may serve you better.

Neither is better. The useful question is what you want from the next ten hours: the speed and variety of a series, or the depth and sustained argument of a book.

## A sensible way to decide

Rather than guess, run a simple test.

1. **Pick one audiobook you would buy anyway.** Not a "someday" title, one you actually want.
2. **Check whether your library has it.** If it does, borrow it and see how you get on with the format.
3. **If you finish it and want another, work out the cost.** Compare the current terms of a membership with the price of the next two or three books you have in mind, using the live prices on Audible's page.
4. **Set a reminder if you do sign up.** A membership that renews until cancelled is easy to forget, and the terms are Audible's, so read them.

If that test tells you audiobooks are for you, a membership is the more convenient route. If not, you have lost nothing.

## The bottom line

If you finish books, want specific titles and have time to fill, Audible is likely to be a good fit. If you are happy with podcasts or can borrow from a library, you may not need it. The best test is cheap and quick: pick one book, see whether you finish it, and go from there.
