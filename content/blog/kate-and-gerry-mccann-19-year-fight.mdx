No parents in modern British history have had their private grief scrutinised quite like Kate and Gerry McCann's. In the nineteen years since their daughter Madeleine vanished from a holiday apartment in Praia da Luz, the couple have been treated by Portuguese police as formal suspects, sued a retired detective for libel and lost, been publicly apologised to by that same police force, and — as recently as 2025 — sat behind a courtroom privacy screen as prosecution witnesses in the trial of a woman who insisted she was their missing daughter.

Their story is no longer only about Madeleine's disappearance. It's become a case study in what happens when private tragedy collides, for nearly two decades, with public suspicion, media appetite and the internet's capacity for obsession.

## Two Doctors From Leicestershire

Kate and Gerry McCann were both working as hospital doctors, living in the village of Rothley, Leicestershire, when they took their three children — Madeleine and twins Sean and Amelie — on a package holiday to the Ocean Club in Praia da Luz in April 2007. Nothing about the trip marked them out as anything other than a fairly typical middle-class British family on a spring holiday with friends.

That changed irreversibly on the evening of 3 May 2007, when [Madeleine disappeared](/blog/the-night-madeleine-mccann-disappeared) from the ground-floor apartment where she'd been left sleeping with her siblings while her parents dined with friends nearby. What followed was not just a missing-person investigation but the start of a public role neither of them had trained for: campaigners for their own daughter, watched and judged by an international audience from the first week.

## Named as Arguidos: September 2007

For the first four months of the investigation, Kate and Gerry were treated by Portuguese police as concerned parents. That shifted on 7 and 8 September 2007, when they were formally declared arguidos — official suspects under Portuguese law — a status that gave them certain legal protections but also signalled publicly that detectives suspected their involvement.

Both were questioned at length. Gerry answered most of the questions put to him. Kate, on legal advice, declined to answer any of the 48 questions she was asked over an eleven-hour interrogation. She later said she'd been offered a form of plea deal — an admission of covering up an accidental death in exchange for a reduced sentence — which she refused. The couple flew home to the UK with the twins on 9 September, but the arguido status stayed in place for another ten months, during which the global press routinely referred to Madeleine's own parents as suspects in her disappearance.

<Callout type="warning">
Portugal's attorney general formally lifted the arguido status of Kate, Gerry and Robert Murat on 21 July 2008 and archived the case for lack of evidence. Neither has been named a suspect since, by any authority in any country. That clearance is a matter of official record, not opinion.
</Callout>

## Cleared, But Not Free of Suspicion

Being cleared didn't undo the previous year of coverage. A meaningful share of the public conversation about Madeleine's case — then, and to some extent still now — circles back to theories about parental involvement that Portuguese authorities themselves abandoned in 2008. That gap between an official clearance and a persistent undercurrent of online suspicion has shaped much of what the McCanns have done publicly in the years since. Our [piece on the case's main theories](/blog/madeleine-mccann-theories-explained) covers why that particular theory was rejected in more detail.

## Building the Campaign

Rather than retreat from public life, the McCanns built a structured, long-running campaign. The Official Find Madeleine Campaign and its associated fund financed private investigators, legal costs and public appeals across multiple countries. Kate McCann published a memoir, *Madeleine*, in May 2011, timed to Madeleine's birthday. The couple gave interviews, attended vigils, and lobbied at a political level — pressure from the McCanns, channelled through then-Home Secretary Theresa May with the backing of Prime Minister David Cameron, led to Scotland Yard reviewing the Portuguese investigation in May 2011, the review that became [Operation Grange](/blog/operation-grange-explained) two years later.

Their campaigning has also broadened well beyond their own daughter's case. In recent years the McCanns have partnered with the missing-persons charity Missing People to support Safecall, a confidential helpline for young people at risk of criminal or sexual exploitation, or who have run away from home or care.

## The Amaral Legal Battle

One of the most bruising chapters of the McCanns' campaigning years had nothing to do with Christian Brueckner or any other suspect. It involved Gonçalo Amaral, the Portuguese police inspector who led the original investigation until his removal from the case in October 2007.

In 2008, Amaral published *A Verdade da Mentira* ("The Truth of the Lie"), arguing Madeleine had died in an accident in the apartment and that her parents staged an abduction to conceal it. The McCanns sued for libel, seeking €1.2 million in damages. A Lisbon court initially ruled in their favour in 2015, awarding €500,000. Portugal's Supreme Court overturned that in 2017, finding Amaral's claims were protected under freedom-of-expression law. The McCanns took the case to the European Court of Human Rights, which in September 2022 ruled against them, finding Portugal's courts had given them a fair hearing.

The result is an uncomfortable legal reality that sits alongside the couple's factual clearance as suspects: Amaral's book remains lawfully published in Portugal, even though the criminal investigation that originally produced the suspicion against the McCanns was closed without charge. Being legally entitled to publish an opinion is a different thing from that opinion having been substantiated — no Portuguese or British authority has reopened proceedings against the McCanns since 2008.

## A New Kind of Ordeal: The Julia Wandelt Case

Just as the criminal case against Christian Brueckner moved forward, the McCanns faced an entirely different and deeply personal form of harassment. Between June 2022 and February 2025, a Polish woman named Julia Wandelt, convinced she was Madeleine, repeatedly contacted the couple by phone, email and letter, appeared at their home in Rothley, and approached family friends, insisting on a DNA comparison.

Wandelt, supported by co-defendant Karen Spragg, confronted Kate McCann directly outside the family home in December 2024. Operation Grange's own DNA testing, taken after Wandelt's arrest in February 2025, ruled out any match. Both women were tried at Leicester Crown Court in late 2025; Kate and Gerry gave evidence from behind a privacy screen. In November 2025, a jury acquitted both women of stalking but found Wandelt guilty of harassment — she was sentenced to six months, already served, with a restraining order. Spragg was acquitted of all charges.

Nearly two decades after Madeleine vanished, her parents are still, in very direct and personal ways, having to defend themselves and their family from the consequences of the case's enduring fame.

## Where Things Stand

In 2023, senior Portuguese police officers travelled to London to meet Gerry McCann and apologise for how the original investigation had been conducted and how the family had been treated — an acknowledgement, sixteen years on, from the same force that once named them suspects. Operation Grange remains funded and active, and its focus in recent years has centred on [Christian Brueckner](/blog/who-is-christian-brueckner-madeleine-mccann), the German man German prosecutors have named as their suspect since 2020. When Portuguese prosecutors formally made him an arguido in April 2022, Kate and Gerry issued a statement calling it "progress in the investigation," while carefully noting that he had "not yet been charged with any specific crime in relation to Madeleine's disappearance" — a distinction the couple, more than anyone else in this case, have reason to insist on.

Nineteen years is a long time to hold a campaign together under that level of scrutiny, legal setback and, at points, direct personal harassment. What the McCanns' story shows, more than any single piece of forensic evidence, is how thoroughly a missing-child case can reshape the lives of the people left behind — not just through grief, but through law, media and the unpredictable attention of strangers.
