**Taylor Parker is a Texas woman on death row for murdering 21-year-old Reagan Simmons-Hancock in New Boston, Texas, on 9 October 2020, and taking her unborn daughter, who died later that day.** For about ten months Parker had pretended to be pregnant. She was convicted of capital murder in October 2022 and sentenced to death the following month. Her appeals to the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals and the US Supreme Court have both failed. Netflix's documentary *Maternal Instinct*, released on 12 June 2026, tells the story.

This guide is for anyone who has watched the film and wants the full case, or wants to understand it before deciding whether to watch. It explains who Reagan was, how the deception worked, what the courts found, where the case stands in November 2026, what the documentary does and doesn't show, and which podcasts are worth your time.

</Callout>

## Who was Reagan Simmons-Hancock?

Reagan Simmons-Hancock was 21 years old and lived in New Boston, a small town in north-east Texas near the Arkansas border. She was married, had a three-year-old daughter, and was about 35 weeks pregnant with her second child, a girl, when she was killed.

Her family have spoken about her warmth and trust in other people, which is part of why the crime hit them so hard. Her mother, Jessica Brookes, attended Parker's trial every day with the rest of the family and appears in the Netflix documentary. The film's director, Jessica Dimmock, has said in an interview with Netflix that "the loss of Reagan should never be forgotten in this story". We agree, and it's why this guide is named for Reagan as much as for Parker.

## Who was Braxlynn Sage Hancock?

Braxlynn Sage Hancock was Reagan's unborn daughter. Parker took her from Reagan's body after the killing. Braxlynn was taken to hospital in Idabel, Oklahoma, where medical staff briefly restored a heartbeat, but she died that day. The question of whether she was born alive became central to the legal case, because it determined whether Parker could be convicted of kidnapping her (see the appeals section below).

You may see other names for the baby in some online summaries. The name used by the courts, Netflix and local reporting is Braxlynn Sage Hancock.

## Who is Taylor Parker?

Taylor Rene Parker, who has also used the surnames Morton and Wacasey, was born in December 1992 and was 27 at the time of the murder. She had two children from earlier relationships, and after the birth of her second child she had a hysterectomy, which meant she could not become pregnant again.

People who knew her describe a long history of lies. Former friends interviewed in the documentary say she repeatedly claimed to be seriously ill. She told people she was the heir to a family fortune, which local reporting says she claimed was tied to the Morton Salt business, and that money from it was coming to her. None of it was true.

In July 2019 she met Wade Griffin, a hog trapper, at a rodeo. She moved in with him in Simms, Texas, that autumn.

## The pregnancy deception

From late 2019, Parker told Griffin, his family and their friends that she was pregnant with his baby. Over roughly ten months she kept the story going with:

- a silicone pregnancy belly;
- sonogram images, which local reporting says she bought online;
- a gender reveal party and a maternity photoshoot;
- a "due date" of 22 September 2020.

As that date passed, Griffin's family and friends grew suspicious. Griffin's mother, Connie, backed her son's doubts, and a family friend, Stephanie Ott, rang clinics and labs to check Parker's story. Both appear in the documentary. Parker's former husband also warned Griffin that she could not be pregnant. According to trial reporting, a planned induction was disrupted first by a fire at Griffin's home and then by a bomb threat to the hospital. Parker then said the birth would happen at a hospital in Idabel, Oklahoma, on 9 October.

Prosecutors later showed the jury her internet searches, which included searches about silicone pregnancy bellies, newborn adoptions and caesarean sections. They argued this showed the killing was planned in advance.

## How did Taylor Parker know Reagan?

Parker worked as a photographer, and she photographed Reagan's wedding in September 2019. The two women stayed in contact afterwards. Reporting on the case says Parker also took an interest in other pregnant women in the area at the time.

## What happened on 9 October 2020

On the morning of 9 October 2020, Parker went to Reagan's home in New Boston. Reagan's three-year-old daughter was in the house. Parker killed Reagan and took her unborn baby, then drove away towards Oklahoma. Reagan was found by her family at the house shortly afterwards.

Later that morning, a Texas state trooper stopped Parker near De Kalb, a few miles west of New Boston. She told him she had just given birth by the roadside and that the baby wasn't breathing. Bodycam and dashcam footage from that stop, which the documentary uses, shows the trooper and paramedics trying to help. Parker and the baby were taken to hospital in Idabel, Oklahoma, where Braxlynn died.

At the hospital, staff established that Parker had not given birth and had no uterus. By then investigators knew about the killing in New Boston. Parker was arrested for murder that day.

## The investigation and the charges

Parker was interviewed by an Oklahoma State Bureau of Investigation agent at her hospital bedside, returned to Texas after waiving extradition, and was booked into jail in Texarkana in mid-October 2020. Wade Griffin was questioned by police; he was never charged and has said the case "pretty much ruined" his reputation.

- **December 2020:** A Bowie County grand jury indicted Parker for capital murder.
- **22 January 2021:** The district attorney announced the state would seek the death penalty.
- **March 2021:** A second capital murder count was added relating to Braxlynn.

Her lawyers asked for the trial to be moved out of Bowie County because of publicity. The judge refused, and jury selection took most of the summer of 2022.

## The trial and conviction

The guilt phase began in Bowie County on 12 September 2022, before Judge John Tidwell. The prosecution's case, led by Kelley Crisp, rested on:

- **the deception:** months of evidence that Parker had faked a pregnancy and was running out of time to produce a baby;
- **premeditation:** her internet searches and the fact that she arrived at Reagan's home equipped to take the baby;
- **her own account** at the roadside and in hospital, which medical evidence immediately disproved.

Parker did not plead insanity, a point the director has also made. On 3 October 2022 the jury took about an hour to find her guilty of capital murder.

## The sentencing hearing

Because the state sought the death penalty, the same jury then heard weeks of evidence about punishment, beginning on 12 October.

- **The defence** asked for life without parole. It called experts who testified that Parker had several psychiatric disorders, and an expert who told the jury that "something is very wrong with her brain", describing a frontal lobe condition.
- **The prosecution** called its own experts, who described her behaviour as psychopathy, and presented evidence that while in jail awaiting trial she had made up new accounts of the killing that blamed other people, including an attempt to implicate another inmate.

On 9 November 2022 the jury returned answers requiring a death sentence, and the judge sentenced her to death. At sentencing, Reagan's mother addressed Parker directly in a victim impact statement. Parker became the seventh woman on Texas death row, and the first woman sentenced to death in Texas since 2012.

## Appeals: where the case stands now

Every Texas death sentence is appealed automatically to the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals, the state's highest criminal court.

**The Texas Court of Criminal Appeals (2025).** Parker's lawyers argued, among other things, that Braxlynn had not been "born and alive", so Parker could not have kidnapped her and the capital murder charge could not stand; that the trial should have been moved because of publicity; and that the jury heard prejudicial evidence about her lying, her sexual history and her parenting. On 6 November 2025 the court rejected every point, finding there was enough evidence for a rational jury to conclude that Braxlynn was born alive, and affirmed the conviction and death sentence (case number AP-77,110).

**The US Supreme Court (2026).** Parker's lawyers petitioned the Supreme Court on 6 March 2026, arguing she had not had a fair trial. The court denied the petition without comment on 18 May 2026.

**What comes next.** With her direct appeals exhausted, Parker can pursue habeas corpus proceedings, first in the Texas courts and then federally, which typically take years. A state habeas case in Bowie County was reported in 2026 to be pending; we have not been able to confirm its current stage. Texas does not usually set an execution date until these are complete. The Texas Department of Criminal Justice's [list of scheduled executions](https://www.tdcj.texas.gov/death_row/scheduled_executions.html), last updated on 20 November 2026, does not include her.

**Where is Taylor Parker now?** She is held on death row at the Patrick L. O'Daniel Unit in Gatesville, Texas, where the state's condemned women are housed. She is in her early thirties, which makes her the youngest woman on Texas's death row.

## The impact on Reagan's family

The crime took Reagan's life and her daughter's, and it left a three-year-old girl without her mother. Reagan's family sat through every day of the trial. In October 2022 her widower filed a wrongful death lawsuit against Parker and Wade Griffin; we have not found reporting on its outcome.

Since the documentary, Reagan's mother and sister have spoken publicly about wanting changes to the law so that medical professionals can warn people when a patient is faking a pregnancy. Dimmock has made the same point in interviews, saying that medical privacy rules "failed Reagan". Whether such a change is workable is a real debate, but it's worth knowing that it's the family's own priority, not just a documentary talking point.

## Netflix's Maternal Instinct: what the documentary covers

| | |
|---|---|
| **Title** | *Maternal Instinct* |
| **Released** | 12 June 2026, worldwide |
| **Format** | Documentary film, 1 hour 37 minutes |
| **Director** | Jessica Dimmock (*The Texas Killing Fields*) |
| **Production company** | Story Syndicate, the company of Liz Garbus and Dan Cogan |
| **UK availability** | [On Netflix UK](https://www.netflix.com/gb/title/81766093), rated 15 |

*Maternal Instinct* is a single feature-length film, not a series. It opens with police footage from the roadside stop, then tells the story of Parker's relationship with Wade Griffin and the months of deception that led to 9 October.

It is told almost entirely through the people who were taken in by Parker or hurt by her:

- **Wade Griffin**, her former boyfriend;
- **Connie**, his mother, and **Stephanie Ott**, a family friend, who both tried to check Parker's story;
- **McKenzie Bright and Abby Bell**, former friends from another town, who describe years of her claims of illness;
- **Jessica Brookes**, Reagan's mother.

**Taylor Parker is not interviewed.** Dimmock has said the filmmakers approached Parker through her lawyers shortly before release and they declined to comment, and that the film deliberately tells "Taylor's ride from other people's perspective".

### Is Maternal Instinct worth watching?

Our assessment draws on Netflix's own material, the director's interviews and the critical coverage, including the podcasts reviewed below.

<Callout type="verdict">
**Yes, with a warning.** It is the only feature-length film on the case, it keeps Parker herself off screen, and it gives Reagan's mother space to speak about her daughter. It also uses real police bodycam footage from the aftermath, which many viewers will find very hard to watch. If you want to understand how the deception worked, it's the best single place to start. If you want the legal story or more about Reagan, you'll need the podcasts below as well.
</Callout>

**What it does well.** By keeping Parker off screen, the film avoids giving her a platform, which in a case like this is the right call. The interviews with Griffin's family and Parker's former friends show how ordinary people talk themselves out of their suspicions, which is the most useful thing a documentary like this can do.

**Where it falls short.** Because it centres the deception, Reagan risks becoming a supporting character in Parker's story, though the interview with her mother partly corrects this. Several of the podcasts below point to what the film leaves out or passes over quickly: the sentencing hearing, Parker's conduct in jail, and the legal question of whether Braxlynn was born alive.

**Who it's for.** Viewers who liked Netflix's *What Jennifer Did* or *American Murder: Laci Peterson*, which Netflix itself compares it to. Who it isn't for: anyone who finds images of real crime scenes or infant resuscitation distressing.

### Important context for viewers

- **Fetal abduction is extremely rare.** Research reported by The Guardian counted 18 cases in the US between 1983 and 2015. The film is not evidence of a wider trend, though Dimmock makes the broader point that pregnant women are most at risk from people close to them.
- **No diagnosis was established before the crime.** Experts at trial disagreed about Parker's mental health, and she did not claim insanity. Be wary of podcasts or videos that confidently diagnose her.
- **Parker's later accounts are not evidence.** Her stories from jail blaming other people were rejected by the jury and the appeals court.
- **The legal process isn't over.** Her conviction is final on direct appeal, but habeas review continues.

## Our view: what this case shows

The most unsettling thing about this case is not the crime, which was rare, but how long the lie lasted. Parker's deception depended on the decency of the people around her: friends who didn't want to accuse someone of faking illness, a boyfriend's family who felt they couldn't demand medical proof, and professionals who couldn't share what they knew. Several people did suspect her, and the documentary's interviews with them show how hard it was to act on that suspicion.

It also raises a question true crime often ducks. Parker has been called a monster in hundreds of episode titles, and her guilt is not in doubt. But the more attention goes to her psychology, the less goes to Reagan, a 21-year-old who was kind to someone she knew from her wedding day. We would rather remember her than diagnose Parker, which is why we've put the episodes that centre Reagan first. For more on that tension, read our piece on [the ethics of true crime podcasting](/blog/ethics-of-true-crime-podcasting).

## The bottom line

Taylor Parker spent ten months pretending to be pregnant, then killed Reagan Simmons-Hancock in her home in New Boston, Texas, on 9 October 2020, and took her unborn daughter, Braxlynn, who died that day. Parker was convicted of capital murder and sentenced to death in 2022. Her conviction was upheld in 2025, the US Supreme Court declined to hear her case in May 2026, and she remains on death row in Gatesville with no execution date. Netflix's *Maternal Instinct* tells how her lies drew people in. Watch it, then listen to Morbidology and to Reagan's mother, so that the story you remember is Reagan's too.
