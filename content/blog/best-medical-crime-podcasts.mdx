Medical crime sits at the intersection of institutional trust and its betrayal. We place extraordinary trust in healthcare professionals — we allow them access to our bodies, our vulnerabilities, our most private information. When that trust is exploited, the resulting crimes are uniquely disturbing because they happen in contexts specifically designed to be safe.

The genre includes everything from surgical incompetence to systematic poisoning to large-scale healthcare fraud. These podcasts cover the full range.

## Dr. Death

The defining medical crime podcast. Dr. Death covers Christopher Duntsch, a Dallas neurosurgeon who harmed or killed dozens of patients through what prosecution argued was intentional malpractice. The show became one of the most downloaded podcasts of 2018 and was subsequently adapted into a Peacock drama series.

What makes Dr. Death compelling is the systemic failure narrative: Duntsch didn't operate in a vacuum. He moved between hospitals, causing catastrophic harm, while regulatory systems failed to stop him. The question of why the medical system protected a dangerous surgeon for so long is as disturbing as Duntsch's individual actions.

Host Laura Beil, a veteran health journalist, provides context that pure true crime shows often lack. She explains the surgical procedures, the regulatory landscape, and the economic incentives that kept Duntsch employed long after he should have been stopped.

## American Scandal: The Bad Doctor

American Scandal dedicates seasons to specific American scandals, and several of its best seasons cover medical topics. The Bad Doctor season follows Larry Nassar, the USA Gymnastics team doctor convicted of sexually abusing hundreds of athletes. The Opioid Crisis season covers the Sackler family's role in creating the opioid epidemic through Purdue Pharma's marketing of OxyContin.

The production quality is high, and American Scandal consistently contextualises individual crimes within institutional failures — how systems enable bad actors, how complaints get ignored, and how money and status protect perpetrators.

## Casefile True Crime: Medical Episodes

Casefile has covered several medical crime cases in its regular rotation, including the Harold Shipman case (the UK GP who murdered 215 patients — the highest confirmed number of murders by a healthcare professional in history), the Beverly Allitt case (a nurse who killed four children in 1991), and the Colin Norris case.

The Shipman episodes in particular are essential. Shipman was Britain's most prolific serial killer by confirmed victim count, and the Casefile treatment is characteristically thorough and restrained.

## Maintenance Phase

Not true crime in the traditional sense, but Maintenance Phase — hosted by Aubrey Gordon and Michael Hobbes — investigates wellness industry fraud and the bad science behind medical and health claims. Episodes on the wellness industry, diet culture, and alternative medicine complement traditional medical crime content by showing how fraud operates when it's legal.

The show is rigorous in a way unusual for non-specialist podcasts: it cites studies, identifies methodological problems, and follows the evidence wherever it leads. For listeners interested in how the healthcare system is manipulated for profit, this is essential alongside more traditionally "criminal" content.

## Swindled

Swindled covers corporate and white-collar crime, and several episodes focus on pharmaceutical and healthcare fraud. The format — hosted by an anonymous "P," in dry, satirical voice — makes corporate crime more engaging than it has any right to be.

Particularly strong on pharmaceutical marketing fraud: the off-label promotion scandals (GlaxoSmithKline's $3 billion settlement, Pfizer's $2.3 billion settlement), the systematic manipulation of clinical trial data, and the financial incentives that drive misprescription. This is the fraud that kills thousands of people per year and receives a fraction of the media attention given to individual killers.

## The Shrink Next Door

A Wondery limited series covering psychiatrist Isaac Herschkopf's 30-year manipulation of patient Marty Markowitz — a relationship in which Herschkopf gradually took control of Markowitz's personal, professional, and financial life. It's not a murder case, but it's one of the best examinations of how therapeutic trust can be exploited.

The show raises questions about what constitutes medical crime when the harm is psychological rather than physical, and about how regulatory systems protect patients (or don't) from practitioner misconduct.

---

## Poisoned: The True Story of the Deadly E. Coli Outbreak

A Wondery podcast about the 1993 Jack in the Box E. coli outbreak, which killed four children and hospitalised 178 others. The investigation covers food safety regulation, corporate negligence, and the fight for stricter food safety laws. It's medical crime in the sense of regulatory failure and public health harm, rather than individual murder.

## Why Medical Crime Is Uniquely Disturbing

There's a psychological specificity to medical crime that makes it different from other categories of crime. Hospitals and doctors' offices are places where we are designed to be more vulnerable than usual — less informed, less physically capable, emotionally distressed. The power differential between patient and healthcare provider is one of the largest in any common human relationship.

When that power differential is exploited — for financial gain, for sadistic satisfaction, or through incompetence enabled by systemic failures — the breach of trust is total. Patients cannot easily identify that something is wrong; by definition, they don't have the expertise to evaluate what's being done to them.

This is why medical crime stories provoke such strong reactions: not just because the harm is serious, but because the conditions that enable it are universal. We will all be patients.
