Podcasts about serial killers are one of the most popular corners of true crime, and one of the most uneven. The same few names (Bundy, Dahmer, Gacy) are retold again and again, often with more interest in the killer's personality than in the people he killed.

The ten shows below are the ones we recommend. We chose them for the quality of their research, for treating victims as people rather than plot points, and for variety: different decades, countries and formats, from single documentary series to a long-running weekly show. Most are serialised, telling one case across several episodes. Each links to its full review with a binge score.

For other kinds of case, see our [best true crime podcasts](/best-true-crime-podcasts) overall. To browse every serial killer show we list, with filters, use the [serial killer podcasts directory](/category/serial-killers).

## The best serialised investigations

### Man in the Window

**Our first pick.** Los Angeles Times reporter Paige St. John tells the story of the man who committed dozens of rapes and at least 13 murders in California in the 1970s and 1980s. Rather than starting with the killer, she starts with the women he attacked, many of whom were not believed at the time. The series follows the case to Joseph James DeAngelo's arrest in 2018, made after investigators used genetic genealogy. It was made with Wondery and runs to 11 episodes.

**Why it's worth your time:** the survivors' interviews, and a clear account of how police failures let the attacks continue.

Our guide to [Golden State Killer podcasts and books](/blog/golden-state-killer-podcasts-and-books) compares it with the other coverage of this case.

### The Clearing

In 2009, April Balascio read about an unsolved 1980 double murder and called the police with a tip: she believed the killer was her father. Edward Wayne Edwards was later convicted, and died in prison in 2011. In this series from Pineapple Street and Gimlet, journalist Josh Dean and Balascio go back through her childhood and the hours of tapes her father recorded about himself. The series also tries to correct the online legend that grew around Edwards, much of it unsupported.

**Why it's worth your time:** it is a family story told from the inside, and it is careful about which claims about Edwards hold up.

### Atlanta Monster

Between 1979 and 1981, at least 28 people were killed in Atlanta, most of them Black children and teenagers. Wayne Williams was convicted in 1982 of murdering two adults and police then closed most of the children's cases, without a trial. Payne Lindsey's series from Tenderfoot TV and HowStuffWorks revisits the case and the doubts raised by victims' families. It is as much about race and policing in Atlanta as about the murders.

**Why it's worth your time:** it puts the families' questions at the centre and doesn't claim to settle them.

### Unraveled: Long Island Serial Killer

Billy Jensen and Alexis Linkletter, both from Long Island, investigated the deaths of women found near Gilgo Beach from 2010. The seven-part season, released in January 2021, looks at the victims, many of whom were sex workers whose disappearances got little attention, and at the troubled police investigation. Two years later, Rex Heuermann was arrested and charged. Listen knowing that the series predates the arrest.

**Why it's worth your time:** a good account of why the case stalled for so long.

### Monster: BTK

Dennis Rader killed ten people in and around Wichita, Kansas, between 1974 and 1991, and sent taunting letters to police and the press. He was caught in 2005 after sending police a floppy disk that could be traced. This 2025 season of iHeartPodcasts and Tenderfoot TV's Monster series covers the investigation, the community that lived with the fear, and the families who lived with it longest.

**Why it's worth your time:** it shows how investigators finally caught him, without making Rader the star.

### Someone's Hunting Us

An NJ.com series by reporters Daysi Calavia-Robertson and Rebecca Everett about Khalil Wheeler-Weaver, who killed women he met through dating apps in New Jersey in 2016. The women he targeted were Black, and police were slow to treat their disappearances seriously. It was a victim's relatives who set up a fake profile to catch him. The final episode, released in 2026, includes conversations with Wheeler-Weaver.

**Why it's worth your time:** a recent, little-known case told by local reporters who covered it from the start.

### Stranglers

Thirteen women were killed in the Boston area between 1962 and 1964. Albert DeSalvo confessed but was never tried for the murders. In 2013, DNA testing linked him to the last victim, Mary Sullivan. Host Portland Helmich's 12-part series, released in 2016, asks whether one man really killed all thirteen, and talks to investigators, journalists and the families who have argued about it for decades.

**Why it's worth your time:** it deals honestly with evidence that points in more than one direction.

### The Nobody Zone

A co-production between RTÉ's Documentary On One and Danish producers Third Ear, written and narrated by Tim Hinman. Kieran Kelly, an Irish-born man living in London, confessed in the 1980s to dozens of killings, many of them deaths of homeless men on the Underground. Some confessions checked out and many could not be proved. The series is a slow, thoughtful look at what happens when a man wants to be seen as a serial killer.

**Why it's worth your time:** it is a rare British and Irish case, and it questions the genre's appetite for big numbers.

## If you want a weekly show

### Serial Killers

Parcast's long-running show, hosted by Greg Polcyn and Vanessa Richardson, covers one killer at a time, usually across two episodes, with a focus on psychology. It is scripted and tightly produced, and it is the easiest serial killer podcast to dip into. Because it covers so many cases, quality and depth vary by episode.

### Jensen & Holes: The Murder Squad

Investigative journalist Billy Jensen and retired investigator Paul Holes, who worked on the Golden State Killer case, discuss unsolved cases and ask listeners to send in tips. Many episodes are about murders that are not serial killings. It is the most practical show here: less storytelling, more how investigations work.

## Responsible listening: what the best shows get right

Many victims' families have said that serial killer podcasts turn their relatives into plot devices. That objection deserves a better answer than just making fewer shows. The best series on this list share three habits:

- **They name and describe the victims as people**, not just as a count.
- **They describe violence only as far as the story needs.** Graphic detail rarely helps you understand a case.
- **They are wary of explaining the killer.** Forensic psychology can describe patterns, but neat stories about how a childhood "created" a killer are usually more satisfying than accurate.

Our article on the [ethics of true crime podcasting](/blog/ethics-of-true-crime-podcasting) goes further into these questions.

## What about Bundy, Dahmer and Gacy?

These cases have been covered more than any others, and most podcast versions add little to the existing books and documentaries. For Ted Bundy, the strongest account is still a book: Ann Rule's *The Stranger Beside Me*, written by a crime writer who knew him.
