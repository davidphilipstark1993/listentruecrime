White collar crime kills fewer people per incident than violent crime, but costs orders of magnitude more money — and often destroys more lives across its full extent. Pension fraud empties retirement accounts. Opioid marketing fraud fuels an addiction crisis that kills 70,000 Americans a year. Ponzi schemes take the savings of people who can't afford to lose them.

The podcasts in this genre are often better journalism than their violent crime equivalents, partly because the evidence is usually documentary (emails, financial records, regulatory filings) rather than forensic, and partly because financial journalists bring specific expertise that general true crime podcasters often lack.

## The Missing Cryptoqueen

BBC journalist Jamie Bartlett's investigation of the OneCoin fraud is the best financial crime podcast ever made. The scheme was astonishing in scale: approximately $4 billion taken from investors worldwide, with the founder — Ruja Ignatova, "the Cryptoqueen" — disappearing in 2017 before justice caught up with her.

What makes this show exceptional is the human element. Bartlett documents not just the fraud but the people it defrauded: intelligent, financially sophisticated people who were manipulated through techniques that exploited specific psychological vulnerabilities. Understanding how people get into these schemes — and why leaving them feels psychologically impossible — is as important as understanding the fraud mechanics.

## Bad Blood: The Final Chapter

The Theranos story — Silicon Valley blood-testing startup, $9 billion valuation, Elizabeth Holmes, total fraud — was covered first in John Carreyrou's Wall Street Journal reporting and his book Bad Blood. The podcast extends that coverage into Holmes' 2021 trial and conviction.

What distinguishes Bad Blood coverage from similar Silicon Valley fraud stories is how long it took anyone to notice. Holmes had fooled some of the most sophisticated investors and board members in the world. Understanding how she did it — and why the normal mechanisms of financial oversight failed — is more useful than simply cataloguing the fraud.

## Swindled

The most entertaining podcast about white collar crime. An anonymous host known as "P" delivers corporate crime stories in a dry, satirical voice that makes regulatory violations and financial fraud compelling in a way that journalism rarely manages.

Swindled covers the full range of corporate crime: pharmaceutical fraud, food industry violations, environmental crime, banking misconduct. The research is real — the show cites primary sources and regulatory filings — but the delivery is comedic in a way that makes previously impenetrable subject matter accessible.

Particularly strong on the opioid crisis, pharmaceutical marketing fraud, and the spectacular failures of financial regulation.

## American Scandal

American Scandal dedicates each season to a specific American corporate or political scandal. The strongest seasons for white collar crime:

- **Theranos**: Elizabeth Holmes' blood-testing fraud
- **The Opioid Crisis**: The Sackler family, Purdue Pharma, and the manufacturing of addiction
- **WeWork**: Adam Neumann's spectacular corporate implosion
- **Enron**: The Texas energy company whose accounting fraud brought down Arthur Andersen

The production quality is consistently high, and host Lindsay Graham contextualises each case within the broader social and regulatory environment. The Opioid Crisis season is particularly important — it documents deliberate manipulation of the medical profession by a pharmaceutical company that knew the risks of its product.

## Ponzi Supernova

A deep dive into Scott Rothstein, a Florida lawyer who ran a $1.2 billion Ponzi scheme through his law firm. The show covers how Rothstein built a network of political connections, charitable giving, and social prestige that insulated him from scrutiny — and how it all collapsed in 2009.

Less well-known than the Madoff and Theranos stories, but a better examination of how Ponzi schemes actually work in practice: the psychology of the perpetrator, the structure of the scheme, and the specific ways investors are recruited and retained.

## Embedded: The Con Queen of Hollywood

NPR's embedded podcast covered the "Con Queen of Hollywood" — an Indonesian man who impersonated Hollywood executives on the phone, convincing aspiring filmmakers to fly to Southeast Asia for fake productions and then extorting them. An unusual fraud case in that it involves voice manipulation and identity impersonation rather than financial documents.

The case illustrates how fraud has evolved in the digital age: what's technically possible with voice mimicry and email spoofing has expanded the toolkit available to fraudsters substantially.

## The Dropout

ABC News journalist Rebecca Jarvis's investigation of Elizabeth Holmes and Theranos. Pre-dates the trial, so lacks the conviction resolution of Bad Blood, but Jarvis's access to former employees and investors gives it depth that court-based coverage can't match.

Six episodes. Well-produced, reliable on the facts, strong on the institutional failures that allowed the fraud to persist.

## Why White Collar Crime Often Goes Unnoticed

The psychology of white collar crime is different from violent crime, and the best podcasts in the genre understand this. Perpetrators rarely think of themselves as criminals. They use euphemism (financial "creativity" rather than fraud, "optimistic" projections rather than lies). They operate in environments where aggression and risk-taking are rewarded, making it genuinely hard to identify when confidence becomes deception.

Victims often don't know they've been victimised until years later. The evidence is technical. The legal standards for intent are different from violent crime. And culturally, we treat financial crime as somehow less serious than violence — despite the fact that its aggregate impact on human welfare frequently exceeds that of the violent crime we pay more attention to.

The podcasts on this list treat these asymmetries seriously. They're not just telling stories about bad people making bad choices — they're investigating the systems that enable those choices and the cultural patterns that make financial predation respectable until it isn't.
