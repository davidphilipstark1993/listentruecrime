Missing persons cases occupy a specific and painful place in the true crime genre. Unlike cases where a body has been found and a crime is established, missing persons investigations often live in uncertainty — families who don't know, law enforcement without enough to act on, cases that stay cold for years or decades.

The best podcasts in this sub-genre treat that uncertainty honestly. They don't fabricate resolution. They document what is known, investigate what might be discovered, and give families a platform to keep attention on cases that might otherwise be forgotten.

---

## Your Own Backyard

The gold standard for missing persons podcasting, and one of the only shows where the investigation directly contributed to a criminal conviction.

In 1996, 19-year-old Kristin Smart disappeared after a party at Cal Poly San Luis Obispo. The case had a prime suspect from the beginning — student Paul Flores, the last person seen with Smart — but it went cold for decades. Chris Lambert, a music teacher and true crime amateur, became obsessed with the case around 2018 and began documenting his investigation.

Lambert spent years interviewing witnesses, reviewing documents, and building a public profile of the case. Tips came in. Attention was paid. In 2021, Paul Flores and his father were arrested. In 2022, Paul Flores was convicted of murder and sentenced to 25 years to life.

The podcast is not slick — Lambert is a teacher, not a broadcaster — but the commitment is total. If you listen to one missing persons podcast, this is it.

## Someone Knows Something

CBC journalist David Ridgen partners with families of missing or murdered people to reinvestigate cold cases. Unlike most true crime podcasts, Ridgen actually works alongside families — he's a documentarian who makes himself part of the investigation, not just a narrator of it.

Each season covers a different case. Highlights:

- **Season 1**: Adrien McNaughton, missing since 1972 at age 5 in Ontario
- **Season 2**: Kerrie Brown, murdered in 1983 in Ontario
- **Season 4**: Sheryl and Grace Dockstader, murdered in 1986

The show is notable for what it doesn't do: it doesn't sensationalise, doesn't push theories, and doesn't try to force resolution on cases that don't have one. Ridgen is a rare figure in true crime — a journalist who seems to genuinely care more about finding answers than making good audio.

## Missing Maura Murray

Maura Murray is one of America's most discussed missing persons — a college student who disappeared in New Hampshire in 2004 after a car accident on Route 112. The case has generated extraordinary online investigation from the true crime community, and hosts Lance Reenstierna and Tim Pilleri are among the longest-running investigators.

The podcast is now over 150 episodes and has become something of a clearinghouse for all investigation into the Murray case. The hosts have been careful to distinguish what is documented from what is speculative — important in a case where theories have proliferated wildly.

Whether Maura Murray will ever be found is unknown. The podcast documents the best available thinking about what might have happened.

## The Vanished

Marissa Jones created The Vanished to give families of missing persons a platform — and that's almost exactly what the show does. Every episode, a family member or close friend describes the missing person, the circumstances of their disappearance, and the investigation (or lack thereof) that followed.

The production is simpler than most shows on this list. There are no narrative flourishes, no expert interviews, no dramatic music. Just families talking about people who are gone.

This simplicity is the show's greatest quality. Missing persons cases don't resolve neatly; The Vanished doesn't pretend they do.

## In the Red Clay

Georgia-based journalist Pamela Colloff (known for her work at Texas Monthly and ProPublica) is one of the best crime journalists working in any medium. This podcast covers the disappearance of 9-year-old Asha Degree in North Carolina on Valentine's Day 2000. Asha was seen walking alone on Highway 18 in the middle of the night during a storm — and then was gone.

The podcast brings Colloff's meticulous journalism to a case that has never made national headlines despite being one of the most mysterious disappearances in recent American history. A book bag belonging to Asha, buried in plastic, was found two years after her disappearance. What happened to Asha herself has never been established.

## Over My Dead Body: Miracle Man

Not a missing persons case in the traditional sense, but this season of Wondery's Over My Dead Body covers a case with sustained uncertainty about what actually happened. Worth including because the investigative approach — what do we know, what remains unknown — is similar to the best missing persons shows.

## Missing & Murdered: Finding Cleo

A Canadian podcast from CBC about the residential school system and the ongoing crisis of murdered and missing Indigenous women and girls. The host, Connie Walker, is from Saulteaux First Nation and brings a deeply personal perspective to a case that mainstream true crime has largely ignored.

Finding Cleo focuses on Cleo Nicotine, an Indigenous child from Saskatchewan who was forcibly removed from her family and placed in the American foster care system in the 1970s. What happened to her is documented with care and with genuine outrage at the systems that made such outcomes inevitable.

This is essential listening, and not just for missing persons fans. It's among the most important true crime podcasts ever made.

## Casefile: Selected Episodes

Casefile covers many missing persons cases alongside its broader true crime scope. Episodes on the disappearance of Madeleine McCann, the Beaumont Children, and several high-profile Australian missing persons cases are among the best the genre has produced on those cases.

If you're looking for missing persons content as part of a broader rotation rather than a dedicated missing persons show, Casefile is the best single podcast to add.

## A Note on Ethics

Missing persons cases involve living families in a way that closed murder cases sometimes don't. The families of people who are still missing haven't had a resolution. They are still in grief, still holding hope, still potentially contactable by listeners who think they have a theory.

The best missing persons podcasts treat this carefully. They don't encourage amateur "investigation" that could harm families. They correct theories that have circulated online. They show the human cost of attention that turns into harassment.

If you develop strong feelings about a case — as many listeners do — the appropriate channel is always through official law enforcement, not online speculation.
