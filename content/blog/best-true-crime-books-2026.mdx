Podcasts are very good at some things: voices, pace, the sense of an investigation unfolding in real time. Books are better at others. A good true crime book can hold far more detail, correct itself in footnotes, and give the people at the centre of a case more room than an episode ever can.

This list is for podcast listeners who want to read something next. None of these books is new — the most recent was published in 2019 — and that is deliberate. These are the books we think are most worth your time in 2026: accounts that have held up, or whose flaws are well enough understood that you can read them with open eyes. Where a book has a significant problem, we say so.

For each book, we've noted who it suits and, where there's a genuine connection, a podcast to pair it with.

## Serial killers and the people who pursued them

### The Stranger Beside Me — Ann Rule

Ann Rule was a former Seattle police officer writing for true crime magazines when she volunteered at a crisis helpline in the early 1970s. One of her fellow volunteers was a young psychology student named Ted Bundy. Some years later, when she was under contract to write about a series of murders of young women in the Pacific Northwest, the investigation she was following turned towards the man she knew.

That coincidence is what makes the book unusual. Rule is writing as a crime reporter and as someone who liked Bundy and trusted him, and she is candid about how slowly she accepted what the evidence showed. It remains one of the most widely read accounts of the Bundy case, and our [serial killer podcast guide](/blog/best-serial-killer-podcasts) suggests it over most of the podcasts about him.

**Best for:** readers who want the definitive account of the Bundy case, and anyone interested in how people close to an offender fail to see him.

### Mindhunter — John E. Douglas and Mark Olshaker

John Douglas was one of the FBI agents who, in the 1970s and 1980s, interviewed convicted serial killers in prison to try to understand how they thought and behaved, and used what they learned to build the profiling methods the Bureau's Behavioral Science Unit became known for. *Mindhunter*, written with Mark Olshaker and published in 1995, is his account of that work. It was the basis for the Netflix series of the same name.

It is a memoir, and reads like one: Douglas is the hero of his own story, and criminal profiling has since faced serious scrutiny from researchers who question how reliable it is. Read it as a first-hand account of how the FBI came to think about serial offenders, not as settled science.

**Best for:** listeners who enjoy the psychology-focused end of serial killer podcasting, and anyone who watched the Netflix series and wants the source.

## Cold cases

### I'll Be Gone in the Dark — Michelle McNamara

Michelle McNamara spent years investigating a series of rapes and murders committed across California in the 1970s and 1980s, by an offender she gave the name the Golden State Killer. She died in 2016 before finishing the book. It was completed by her lead researcher, Paul Haynes, and the journalist Billy Jensen, with her husband, Patton Oswalt, and published in February 2018.

Two months later, Joseph James DeAngelo was arrested after investigators used genetic genealogy to identify him. In 2020 he pleaded guilty to thirteen murders and was sentenced to life without parole. The book does not describe the arrest; it describes the long years before it, and one person's determination to keep the case alive. That is exactly why it is worth reading.

**Best for:** cold case listeners, and anyone who has followed the Golden State Killer case. Pair it with [The BG Files](/podcasts/the-bg-files), the podcast from Paul Holes, one of the investigators who worked the case, and our [Golden State Killer case page](/cases/golden-state-killer).

### The Cases That Haunt Us — John E. Douglas and Mark Olshaker

The second book on this list from Douglas and Olshaker takes a different approach: a series of famous unsolved or disputed cases — including Jack the Ripper, Lizzie Borden, the [Zodiac Killer](/cases/zodiac-killer) and [JonBenét Ramsey](/cases/jonbenet-ramsey) — examined through the eyes of a former profiler.

It was published in 2000, so it predates later developments in several of these cases, and some of its conclusions are opinions rather than findings. As a way into how investigators think about cold cases, it still works well.

**Best for:** readers who like the case-by-case format of episodic podcasts. Our [cold case podcast guide](/blog/best-cold-case-podcasts) is a good companion.

### The Murder Room — Michael Capuzzo

The Vidocq Society is a members' club in Philadelphia, founded in 1990, whose detectives, forensic scientists and other specialists meet over lunch to review unsolved cases brought to them by police and families. Michael Capuzzo's book follows three of its founders and several of the cases they took on.

It is more of a group portrait than a single-case investigation, and it sits at the lighter end of this list. For listeners who enjoy the procedural detail of weekly cold case shows such as [The Trail Went Cold](/podcasts/the-trail-went-cold), it is an absorbing read.

**Best for:** readers interested in how cold cases are actually reopened, and in the people who do that work.

## International and British true crime

### People Who Eat Darkness — Richard Lloyd Parry

In July 2000, Lucie Blackman, a 21-year-old former British Airways flight attendant working in Tokyo, disappeared. Richard Lloyd Parry, then Tokyo correspondent for *The Times*, followed the case for more than a decade: the search, the discovery of her remains, the investigation of a wealthy businessman, Joji Obara, and the trials that followed. Obara was never convicted of her murder, although he was found guilty of charges connected to her abduction and the disposal of her body, and of a series of other sexual assaults.

This is serious, patient journalism. Lloyd Parry is careful with Lucie Blackman's family, whose own conflicts are part of the story, and clear about how the Japanese justice system handled the case. It is one of the best examples of the genre written in the last twenty years.

**Best for:** listeners who value investigative journalism over storytelling for its own sake. If you've listened to [Death in Ice Valley](/podcasts/death-in-ice-valley), this has a similar seriousness about a death abroad.

### The Five — Hallie Rubenhold

*The Five* is about the women killed in Whitechapel in 1888 — Mary Ann Nichols, Annie Chapman, Elizabeth Stride, Catherine Eddowes and Mary Jane Kelly — and it deliberately says almost nothing about their killer. Hallie Rubenhold reconstructs their lives from archival records, showing who they were before the autumn they became known only as victims.

It won the Baillie Gifford Prize for Non-Fiction in 2019. Its argument that several of the women were not sex workers has been disputed by some historians of the case, which is worth knowing, but the book's central point — that the women have been reduced to the manner of their deaths — stands regardless.

**Best for:** readers who find the victim-centred approach of the better modern podcasts more interesting than the hunt for the culprit. See also our [British true crime podcast guide](/blog/best-british-true-crime-podcasts).

### The Jigsaw Man — Paul Britton

Paul Britton was a clinical psychologist who worked with British police forces on a number of major investigations in the 1980s and 1990s, and *The Jigsaw Man*, published in 1997, is his account of that work.

It needs to be read with its context. Britton helped design the covert operation that targeted Colin Stagg over the 1992 murder of Rachel Nickell on Wimbledon Common. In 1994 the judge threw out the case against Stagg and strongly criticised the operation. In 2008 another man, Robert Napper, was convicted over Rachel Nickell's death, and Stagg later received compensation. Britton's book was written before Napper was identified.

We include it because it is an important primary source on how psychological profiling entered British policing — and because reading it alongside what came later is a lesson in the risks of that approach.

**Best for:** readers interested in British policing history who are prepared to read critically.

## Classic and historical true crime

### In Cold Blood — Truman Capote

In November 1959, four members of the Clutter family were murdered in their home in Holcomb, Kansas. Truman Capote travelled there with Harper Lee to report the story, and spent years on it. *In Cold Blood* was serialised in *The New Yorker* in 1965 and published as a book in 1966, the year after the two men convicted of the murders, Richard Hickock and Perry Smith, were executed.

Capote called it a "non-fiction novel", and it shaped a great deal of the true crime writing that followed. It is also known to contain scenes and dialogue that Capote altered or invented. Read it as a landmark of the genre and a work of literature — not as a wholly reliable record.

**Best for:** anyone who wants to understand where modern narrative true crime comes from. Pair it with [Cold Blooded: The Clutter Family Murders](/podcasts/cold-blooded-clutter-family), a podcast that revisits the case.

### The Devil in the White City — Erik Larson

Erik Larson's book tells two stories side by side: the building of the 1893 World's Fair in Chicago, led by the architect Daniel Burnham, and the crimes of H. H. Holmes, who killed people in the city while the fair was under way.

It is as much a history of the fair as a crime book, and readers expecting a straightforward murder investigation may find the architecture chapters slow. Some of the more lurid claims about Holmes, particularly the size of his death toll, have since been questioned by historians.

**Best for:** readers of narrative history who want their true crime set in a vividly drawn period.

### Killers of the Flower Moon — David Grann

In the early 1920s, members of the Osage Nation in Oklahoma — made wealthy by oil beneath their land — were murdered in large numbers, and the killings went largely unpunished. David Grann's book, published in 2017, tells how the case became one of the first major investigations of the Bureau of Investigation, the forerunner of the FBI.

Grann's real achievement is in the final part of the book, where he shows that the murders were far more widespread than the cases that reached court. It was adapted into Martin Scorsese's 2023 film.

**Best for:** readers who want investigative history with a clear sense of injustice, rather than a single puzzle to solve.

## Crimes in healthcare

### The Good Nurse — Charles Graeber

Charles Cullen worked as a nurse in hospitals in New Jersey and Pennsylvania for sixteen years before his arrest in 2003. He admitted killing dozens of patients; the true number is unknown. Charles Graeber's book, published in 2013, examines how Cullen was able to move between hospitals for so long, and how a fellow nurse, Amy Loughren, helped detectives bring the case to an end. It was adapted as a 2022 Netflix film.

Its most troubling material is institutional: what hospitals knew, and what they chose not to pursue. That makes it relevant to other cases in which healthcare workers have been suspected of harming patients, though each case is different and should be judged on its own facts.

**Best for:** readers interested in how institutions respond to suspicion. [Dr. Death](/podcasts/dr-death), about the surgeon Christopher Duntsch, explores a different case with some of the same questions, as does our case page on the British GP [Harold Shipman](/cases/harold-shipman).
