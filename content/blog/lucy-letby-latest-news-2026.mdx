Three years after her first conviction, Lucy Letby's case is still generating significant news, on several fronts at once. In the space of a few months in 2026 alone: prosecutors confirmed she will not face further charges over new allegations, a family legal challenge to that decision was rejected, a doctor whose relationship with Letby featured heavily at her trial has died, and her Criminal Cases Review Commission application has continued to grow, well over a year after it was first lodged.

Few closed criminal cases continue to produce this volume of genuine, substantive news years after sentencing — a signal of how unusual this case has become. Most whole-life prisoners disappear from public reporting entirely once appeals are exhausted; Letby's case has instead generated an ongoing sequence of institutional processes — a CCRC review, a [statutory public inquiry](/blog/countess-of-chester-nhs-failures), a separate corporate manslaughter investigation, and periodic CPS decisions on further potential charges — each moving at its own pace.

## The CPS Decision: No Further Charges Over Nine More Babies

In late 2025, Cheshire Police submitted a file of evidence to the Crown Prosecution Service relating to new allegations concerning nine further babies, at both the Countess of Chester Hospital and, notably, Liverpool Women's Hospital, where Letby had trained earlier in her career. On 30 January 2026, the CPS announced it would not bring further charges, concluding that the evidential test required to prosecute had not been met. Malcolm McHaffie, head of the CPS's Special Crime Division, confirmed the decision publicly, stating that no further criminal charges should be brought "relating to deaths and non-fatal collapses of babies" at either hospital. Letby's legal team seized on the decision as further support for their broader argument that the original case rested on weaker evidential ground than the convictions suggested.

Some families connected to the babies covered by that review sought to legally challenge the decision, using the Victims' Right to Review scheme. On 6 August 2026, the CPS confirmed it was rejecting that challenge, concluding its original assessment had correctly applied the evidential test. For the families involved, this closed a difficult and separate chapter from the original trial — one in which they had hoped for formal legal acknowledgement of harm the original prosecution hadn't covered.

<Callout type="warning">
Being handed a file to review, or a decision not to charge, says nothing about the safety of Letby's existing convictions — they're separate legal questions entirely. As of August 2026, her 15 convictions remain the legal record.
</Callout>

## Doctor A: Death of a Central Trial Witness

In early August 2026, it was reported that a doctor who had given evidence at Letby's trial had died, in his fifties, following an unspecified illness. He was known in coverage of the criminal trial as Doctor A, and by a different designation during the subsequent Thirlwall Inquiry — anonymity orders protecting his identity, granted originally to protect his family, have remained in place even after his death, and this article follows the same convention.

His relationship with Letby was a significant feature of both proceedings. The two worked together as a paediatric registrar and neonatal nurse, and the Thirlwall Inquiry heard that they exchanged more than 1,300 messages on Facebook over a few months in 2016. Prosecutors pointed to notes in which Letby referred to him as her "love," arguing her attachment to him was relevant context for the jury, without ever suggesting he had any knowledge of, or involvement in, her offending. His death doesn't affect the legal status of Letby's convictions, but it removes one of the case's more closely watched figures from any future proceedings, including a potential future appeal hearing.

## The CCRC Review: A Year and a Half of Submissions

The Criminal Cases Review Commission's review of Letby's convictions, formally opened in February 2025, remained active eighteen months on. The commission's own published timeline shows a steady stream of expert reports and legal submissions arriving well into 2026, including a formal waiver of legal privilege granted in December 2025 giving the commission access to Letby's original defence files — a step that matters procedurally, since without it the CCRC's ability to test whether her original trial team made reasonable decisions based on the evidence available at the time would have been significantly limited.

The CCRC has been notably careful in its public statements, repeatedly stressing that it is not its role to determine guilt or innocence, only to assess whether new evidence or argument creates a "real possibility" the Court of Appeal would now find the convictions unsafe. The commission's chair issued a public statement in early 2026 specifically to address what it called "possible misunderstandings" about how long the review was taking, setting out a detailed timeline to demonstrate the delay reflected the volume of material still arriving rather than a lack of progress. Full detail on what that material actually consists of is in [our piece on the challenge to her convictions](/blog/is-lucy-letby-innocent).

## The Thirlwall Inquiry: Repeated Delays

The separate statutory inquiry into how the Countess of Chester Hospital handled the case has faced its own series of delays. Early expectations pointed to a report by late 2025; by mid-2026, the inquiry's own guidance suggested publication was unlikely before the parliamentary summer recess had concluded, pushing the realistic timeline to September 2026 at the earliest. Families' representatives have publicly expressed frustration at the repeated slippage, arguing that recommended reforms to NHS whistleblowing practice are being delayed by the very process meant to produce them.

## Netflix and the Renewed Public Debate

Public interest in the case was given a significant boost in February 2026 with the release of a Netflix documentary examining both the original investigation and the subsequent challenge to Letby's convictions, featuring previously unseen footage from her arrest and interviews with families on both sides of the debate. Critical reception was mixed, with some reviewers praising the film's attempt to present the original case and the expert challenge with roughly equal weight, and others arguing the format risked presenting a genuine, evidence-based legal review process as though it were simply "two sides" of an ordinary debate.

## What to Watch For Next

Three processes remain genuinely open as of August 2026, each on its own timeline: the CCRC's review of Letby's convictions, which shows no sign of concluding imminently; the Thirlwall Inquiry's final report, now expected no earlier than September 2026; and Cheshire Police's separate corporate manslaughter investigation into the hospital trust itself. None of these processes is likely to move quickly, and none guarantees a particular outcome. What is clear is that, unlike most concluded criminal cases, Lucy Letby's continues to generate genuine news nearly three years after her first conviction.
