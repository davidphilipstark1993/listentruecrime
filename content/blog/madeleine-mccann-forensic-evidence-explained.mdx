Of all the elements of the Madeleine McCann case, the forensic evidence is probably the most misunderstood. Headlines throughout 2007 and 2008 spoke confidently of "Madeleine's blood" and "Madeleine's DNA," language that implied a level of certainty the underlying science never delivered. Understanding exactly what the forensic record actually shows — and where the gap between reporting and results was widest — matters for a reason beyond historical accuracy: it's what tipped Kate and Gerry McCann from grieving parents into formal suspects in September 2007.

## Why 2007-Era Forensic Technology Struggled With This Case

Standard DNA profiling techniques of the time typically required a reasonably intact, sufficiently sized biological sample. Degraded samples, tiny quantities, or material exposed to heat, moisture or time could easily fall below that threshold, producing partial profiles or no usable result at all. Praia da Luz in May 2007 was, forensically speaking, a difficult environment: a warm coastal climate, a well-used holiday apartment with multiple previous occupants, and — as covered in our [piece on the original investigation](/blog/portuguese-police-investigation-madeleine-mccann) — a crime scene that wasn't immediately secured.

## The Cadaver Dogs

In late July 2007, British police brought in two specialist dogs handled by Martin Grime of South Yorkshire Police: Eddie, an Enhanced Victim Recovery dog trained to detect the scent of human cadaveric material, and Keela, trained to detect trace human blood. Both worked apartment 5A and several vehicles connected to the case.

Eddie reportedly alerted behind a sofa in the living room, near a wardrobe in the McCanns' bedroom, and on Madeleine's soft toy, Cuddle Cat. Keela's alerts pointed to possible blood traces in some of the same locations and in the boot of the Renault Scenic the McCanns had rented.

<Callout type="info">
A trained dog's alert is an indicator that specialist forensic testing is warranted — not a finding in itself. Grime was consistent on this point both at the time and since: the dogs are, in his own description, a tool to guide investigators toward material worth testing, not a substitute for that testing. Canine detection is also imperfect by nature — a dog can alert to residual scent from a source no longer present, or be affected by cross-contamination in an apartment that had housed other holidaymakers, including other families with children, both before and after the McCanns' stay.
</Callout>

## The Renault Scenic and the Timing Problem

The hire car has always been the most contested single piece of physical evidence in the case, for a simple reason: the McCanns rented the Renault Scenic 25 days after Madeleine disappeared. Any trace evidence connecting Madeleine to that specific vehicle would need an explanation for how it got there so long afterward — transferred via clothing or luggage the family continued to use, for instance — rather than being straightforward evidence of the car being used at the time of the disappearance itself.

Forensic examination of the Scenic recovered more than two hundred hairs, fibres and fragments. The majority didn't match reference hair samples taken from Madeleine. Around fifteen blonde or fair hair fragments showed some similarity, but were judged too short for a solid comparison, and too short even for mitochondrial DNA testing — a less demanding technique than standard nuclear DNA profiling. Four of those fifteen were sent for a more sensitive technique: Low Copy Number DNA profiling.

## Low Copy Number DNA: A Technique Under a Cloud

The timing here matters. LCN profiling had been used by the UK's Forensic Science Service since 1999, but its reliability came under intense public scrutiny in December 2007 — the same period the McCann samples were being processed — after a Belfast court ruled LCN evidence inadmissible in a case connected to the 1998 Omagh bombing. The Crown Prosecution Service suspended use of the technique nationally for several weeks while a review took place. That review, published in April 2008, ultimately concluded LCN profiling was "robust" and "fit for purpose," while making 21 recommendations for improving how results were interpreted and reported. In other words, the technique used to analyse the McCann case's most sensitive hair samples was, at that exact moment, at the centre of a separate national controversy about how confidently its results could be trusted.

## What Detectives Told the McCanns vs What the Science Said

One of the more revealing details in the case's forensic record only became public when the Portuguese police files were released in August 2008. On 3 September 2007, a forensic scientist with the Forensic Science Service's major incidents team emailed Portuguese detectives to say it was impossible to conclude whether recovered material had come from Madeleine specifically. Four days later, Kate and Gerry McCann were formally named arguidos, with detectives citing DNA evidence as grounds for suspicion — language considerably more definite, according to the McCanns' own account of their interviews, than the forensic scientist's own written assessment had supported days earlier.

This gap between what the forensic science actually established and what was communicated during questioning is arguably the clearest example in the entire case of forensic ambiguity hardening into apparent certainty on its way from the laboratory to the interview room.

## The Samples That Remain in Storage

More than 600 forensic samples taken from the apartment in 2007 — hair, saliva and other trace material — remain in storage at a laboratory in Coimbra, Portugal. One sample, saliva found on Madeleine's pillow the day after she disappeared, couldn't be resolved into a full DNA profile using the technology available at the time, and its origin has never been established. Scotland Yard requested permission to retest samples in 2012; the request was refused. German investigators, pursuing their case against [Christian Brueckner](/blog/who-is-christian-brueckner-madeleine-mccann), have separately sought access to the same pillow sample, hoping modern techniques might extract a usable profile.

## What the Forensic Record Actually Supports

Strip away two decades of headlines, and the forensic picture that remains is genuinely inconclusive: canine alerts never fully confirmed by laboratory testing, hair evidence too degraded for definitive analysis using 2007-era techniques, a DNA methodology that was itself under formal review at the time it mattered most, and hundreds of samples still sitting untested to modern standards. None of this proves any specific theory of what happened to Madeleine. What it does show is why no prosecutable case has ever been built from the physical evidence gathered in 2007 — and why renewed forensic testing, if it ever happens, remains one of the few realistic paths left toward a definitive answer.
