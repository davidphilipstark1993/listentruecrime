**Who Killed the Banker's Widow? is a Netflix documentary about the murder of María del Carmen Martínez, widow of a former president of the Spanish savings bank CAM, who was shot twice in the head at a family car dealership in Alicante on 9 December 2016.** Her son-in-law, Miguel López, was the only person ever charged. A jury acquitted him in 2019, and after a six-year legal fight that went to Spain's Constitutional Court, the acquittal is final. Nobody has been convicted. The documentary arrives on Netflix on 4 December 2026.

The case is famous in Spain, where it is known as *el crimen de la viuda de la CAM* (the murder of the CAM widow) or the *caso Sala*, but almost nothing has been written about it in English. This guide explains who she was, what happened, why the only suspect walked free, what the documentary is and isn't, and the podcasts worth hearing.

## Who was María del Carmen Martínez?

María del Carmen Martínez, known to family as Mari Carmen, was the widow of Vicente Sala, an Alicante businessman who had been president of the Caja de Ahorros del Mediterráneo. CAM was one of the region's most important institutions until it collapsed in Spain's banking crisis and was taken over by the Bank of Spain in 2011. Sala died in 2011, according to ESdiario's account of the case.

The Sala family was one of Spain's wealthiest, with business interests that included the Novocar car dealership in Alicante. EL PAÍS Audio's investigation describes a family that lived and worked side by side, and that had split into two camps after Vicente Sala's death.

At the centre of that split was what Spanish reporting calls the *acción de oro*, or golden share. Sala's estate was divided among his children, but his widow was left a share that gave her the deciding voice in key decisions about the family's property. She wanted to pass it to her eldest son, Vicente Sala Martínez, who ran the companies. Her three daughters opposed her. According to the EL PAÍS series, she had also cut her daughters out of her will beyond the minimum share that Spanish law reserves for children. One side of the family stayed close to her; the other, by that account, barely spoke to her.

That family conflict is the backdrop to everything that followed. It is also why the case has always been treated in Spain as more than a single crime: it became a public trial of a powerful family.

## What happened on 9 December 2016

On the evening of Friday 9 December 2016, María del Carmen Martínez went to the Novocar dealership to collect her Porsche Cayenne, which had been left in the car-wash bay. Her son-in-law, Miguel López, who is married to one of her daughters and managed the dealership, was there that afternoon and left a few minutes before she was found.

Shortly before 7pm, a dealership employee found her standing beside her car, covered in blood and unable to speak. She had been shot twice in the head. She died of her injuries. No one reported seeing the shooting, and the gun was never recovered.

Because of where it happened and who was there, police and the media focused on the family almost immediately.

## The investigation and the arrest of Miguel López

About two months after the murder, police arrested Miguel López. He entered pre-trial detention on 8 February 2017, according to ESdiario. Investigators had assembled 23 *indicios*, or circumstantial indications, pointing to him. After 39 days in custody he was released on bail, which the EL PAÍS series notes is very unusual for someone accused of murder in Spain.

The case against him, as presented by prosecutors and the private prosecution led by the victim's eldest son, rested on circumstance rather than direct evidence:

- **Opportunity.** López ran the dealership, had been there with the victim shortly before the shooting and had left minutes before she was found.
- **The family feud.** He was married to one of the daughters on the opposing side of the inheritance dispute.
- **Firearms.** He had experience of sport shooting, and police found several weapons when they searched his home. He was also charged with illegal possession of a weapon.
- **The location.** Prosecutors argued that the car-wash bay was chosen because its cameras did not work. The defence said the site had never had cameras at all, only motion sensors.

The public prosecutor sought a sentence of around 24 years.

## The defence

López's lawyers argued that there was no physical evidence linking him to the shooting, and the jury later agreed:

- **Gunshot residue.** A test on López for gunshot residue came back negative.
- **No forensic traces.** None of his DNA or other traces were found at the scene. Investigators analysed his computer, tablet and phone and took DNA samples from dozens of men who had been at the dealership, and nothing incriminating was reported.
- **No witness.** Nobody testified to seeing him go to the car-wash bay with her.
- **No weapon.** The gun was never found, so it could not be linked to any firearm he owned.
- **No financial motive, in their account.** The defence said he was married under a separation-of-property regime and stood to gain nothing directly from the inheritance.

López did not testify at his trial. He has always denied killing his mother-in-law, and he gave his own account publicly for the first time in the EL PAÍS Audio podcast in 2023.

## The trial and the verdict (2019)

López was tried before a jury at the Audiencia Provincial in Alicante, with Judge Francisca Bru presiding. Spanish jury trials require the jury to give written reasons for its verdict, and that rule shaped everything that came after.

The jury's first verdict form was sent back by the judge, who found that it was not properly reasoned. Shortly afterwards, in November 2019, the jury returned a verdict of not guilty by six votes to three. It said there was no proof that López had fired the shots, and that nobody had seen him accompany the victim to the car wash. He was acquitted of both murder and illegal weapon possession.

What the first verdict form said has never been made public. It was later destroyed, and that became the basis of years of appeals.

## The appeals: from acquittal to retrial and back again

**March 2020: the Valencian High Court upholds the acquittal.** The Tribunal Superior de Justicia de la Comunitat Valenciana rejected appeals from both the public prosecutor and the private prosecution.

**May 2022: the Supreme Court orders a retrial.** The victim's eldest son took the case to Spain's Supreme Court, which annulled the acquittal. It held that the way the judge had handled the first verdict form, and the destruction of that form, had irreparably damaged the private prosecution's right of defence, because it was now impossible to know what the jury had originally decided or why. It ordered a new trial before a different jury.

**October 2025: the Constitutional Court restores the acquittal.** López took the case to the Constitutional Court on fundamental-rights grounds, and the retrial was suspended while it decided. On 6 October 2025 the court's First Chamber ruled that the defects the Supreme Court identified did not amount to a constitutionally relevant breach of rights. The judge had called a specific hearing with the parties and the jury before returning the form, and the private prosecution had raised no objection at the time. The court annulled the Supreme Court's ruling and declared the 2020 appeal judgment final. Two of the chamber's magistrates dissented.

That ended the criminal case against Miguel López. Under Spanish law he cannot be tried again for the murder.

## Where does the case stand now?

As of November 2026, nearly ten years after her death, no one has been convicted of killing María del Carmen Martínez, and no other suspect has been publicly identified or charged. We have found no reliable report of the police investigation being formally reopened.

Three things are worth keeping separate:

- **What the courts found.** A jury found that the evidence did not prove López shot her, and that verdict is final. In law he is not guilty.
- **What remains unknown.** Who fired the shots, why, and what happened to the gun.
- **What is still argued.** The private prosecution pursued López for years, and the case divided public opinion in Spain. Allegations made against him during the trial were not proven, and he is entitled to be treated as innocent.

The Netflix documentary is reported to explore "new lines of inquiry" arising from the work of journalists Braulio García Jaén and Bárbara Ayuso. What those are has not been made public, and we will not guess.

### What the documentary is likely to cover, and what it may not

Based on what has been announced, the film's strengths should be the reconstruction of the crime scene and the court saga, and the access that comes with the EL PAÍS reporters, who have followed the case since 2016.

Things a single 90-odd-minute film may struggle to cover in depth:

- **The detail of the appeals.** The verdict-form dispute is the heart of the legal story, and it is technical.
- **The family's business history.** The inheritance fight is the context for everything, but the companies and the golden share are complicated.
- **The CAM collapse.** The bank's failure is a separate public scandal, and the title leans on the "banker" angle. Don't expect the documentary to be about banking.

## Our view: why this case is worth understanding

Strip away the wealth and the golden share and the case leaves an uncomfortable fact: a woman was shot twice at a working dealership owned by her own family, and nearly ten years later nobody has been held responsible. That is a failure, whoever you think did it.

The legal story is also a useful lesson for anyone who follows true crime. The fight over López's acquittal was never about new evidence. It was about procedure: a verdict form returned and then destroyed. The Supreme Court and the Constitutional Court reached opposite conclusions about the same facts, and the case ended on whether the private prosecution had objected at the right moment. That is not the kind of ending documentaries like, but it is how criminal justice often works.

Finally, the case shows the cost of being the only suspect. López was acquitted in 2019 but lived under the threat of a retrial until October 2025. A documentary that promises "new lines of inquiry" has to be honest about where that leaves him. We'll be watching for whether it is. For more on that tension, see our piece on [the ethics of true crime podcasting](/blog/ethics-of-true-crime-podcasting).

## The bottom line

María del Carmen Martínez was murdered in Alicante on 9 December 2016. Her son-in-law Miguel López was the only person charged, and his acquittal became final in October 2025 after the Supreme Court's retrial order was overturned. The case is officially unsolved. Netflix's *Who Killed the Banker's Widow?* arrives on 4 December 2026, and it is the first chance for most English-speaking viewers to see the story. Watch it with the court record in mind, and if you speak Spanish, listen to *El enemigo*, which remains the fullest account of the case.
