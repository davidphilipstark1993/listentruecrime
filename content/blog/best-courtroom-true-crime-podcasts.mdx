Courtroom true crime splits into two instincts that don't always sit well together: the appetite for a live, unfolding trial, and the slower, angrier interest in cases where the system got it wrong. The best shows in this category tend to commit fully to one or the other rather than trying to be both. Here are twelve that do.

## For the trial you can't look away from

**Murdaugh Murders Podcast** got there before Netflix did, and its early reporting on South Carolina's Murdaugh family — a century-long dynasty of lawyers and prosecutors, and the 2021 killing of Alex Murdaugh's wife and son — still holds up as the most detailed audio account of how the case actually unravelled. It's dense and occasionally repetitive if you're not from the region, but nothing else gets as close to the local detail.

If your interest runs more current, **The Trial of Erin Patterson** covers the Australian "mushroom murders" case almost as it happened — a woman convicted of killing three of her husband's relatives with a beef Wellington laced with death cap mushrooms. It's a genuinely strange case told with restraint rather than sensationalism, which is harder to pull off with material this bizarre than it sounds.

## For wrongful conviction, done properly

This is where the category is strongest, and where the four shows below each earn their place differently. **In the Dark**'s second season on Curtis Flowers isn't just well-reported — it's journalism that changed a legal outcome: the show's statistical analysis of racially discriminatory jury selection was submitted to the Supreme Court and cited in the 2019 ruling that overturned his conviction. Few podcasts anywhere can claim that kind of direct impact, and it's rated a 10/10 on our scale for it.

**Bone Valley** goes narrower and gets forensic about it — one Florida wrongful-conviction case, taken apart piece by piece, with the kind of patience most shows don't have the runway for. **Wrongful Conviction with Jason Flom** takes the opposite structural approach: a new case, and a new exoneree, every episode, told mostly in the subject's own words. It's less about any single case and more about the sheer accumulation of how often this happens.

**THREE** is the one show on this list where the host has actually lived the thing everyone else is reporting on. Amanda Knox was wrongfully convicted of murder in Italy and later exonerated, and she brings that to bear on the cases she covers — including, in one recent season, the conviction of nurse Lucy Letby. It's not the most polished production here, but it doesn't need to be; the perspective is the value.

## For the courtroom as a window into something bigger

**Believed** uses the Larry Nassar case — hundreds of survivors, one of the largest sexual abuse scandals in American sports history — to examine how an institution (USA Gymnastics, Michigan State) failed to act for two decades despite repeated warnings. It's a hard listen, deliberately so, and it's less about the trial itself than about everything that let it take so long to get there.

<PodcastRecommendation
  slug="believed"
  title="Believed"
  description="The Larry Nassar case as a study in institutional failure — survivor testimony over two decades of warnings that went nowhere."
  rating={9}
  tags={["Institutional Failure", "Survivor Stories"]}
/>

**Suave** won a Pulitzer Prize, and it earns it: a journalist's 27-year relationship with a man sentenced to life without parole as a teenager, following him from prison through his eventual release under a Supreme Court ruling on juvenile sentencing. It's less a "case" podcast than a character study that happens to run through the justice system — genuinely one of the best-produced pieces of audio in the whole genre, courtroom or otherwise.

## For medical and professional crime

**Dr. Death** covers Christopher Duntsch, a Texas surgeon who maimed and killed patients for years before the medical and legal systems that were supposed to stop him finally did. It's as much an indictment of hospital credentialing failures as it is a story about one man, and the courtroom scenes — when they finally arrive — land harder for how long the institutional failure was allowed to run.

## For character-driven trial drama

**The Thing About Pam** doesn't pretend to be neutral about its subject, and that's the point — this is courtroom true crime as character study, built around a genuinely extraordinary figure at the centre of a Missouri murder case. If Serial taught the genre restraint, this is the show willing to let a villain be a villain.

## For the legal analysis itself

If what you actually want is less narrative and more cross-examination of the evidence, **Undisclosed** — three practising attorneys working case by case — and the original **Serial**, which more or less created this entire category by asking whether Adnan Syed's conviction for the murder of Hae Min Lee held up, are still the reference points. Serial's patience with ambiguity — refusing to hand you a tidy verdict — is precisely what a lot of imitators since have failed to replicate.

## Where to start

New to courtroom true crime specifically — not just true crime generally — start with **In the Dark**. It's the clearest example of what this category can do at its best: a podcast that didn't just cover a miscarriage of justice, but helped correct one.
