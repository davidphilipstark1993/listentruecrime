**Netflix's *The Crash* tells the story of Mackenzie Shirilla, an Ohio teenager who drove her car at about 100mph into a brick building in Strongsville on 31 July 2022, killing her boyfriend, Dominic Russo, 20, and their friend Davion Flanagan, 19.** She was 17. A judge found her guilty of murder in 2023, ruling that she crashed deliberately, and she is serving 15 years to life. The documentary, released on 15 May 2026, includes her first interview from prison, in which she insists it was not intentional.

If you've just watched *The Crash*, you'll know it leaves you to sit with an uncomfortable question: how could anyone do this on purpose? This guide sets out what the court actually found and why, what the documentary does well and what it skips, where the case stands now, and the podcast episodes that are worth your time.

The facts here come from the [Eighth District Court of Appeals' ruling](https://www.supremecourt.ohio.gov/rod/docs/pdf/8/2024/2024-Ohio-4674.pdf), which sets out the trial evidence in detail, the [Cuyahoga County Prosecutor's Office](https://www.ccprosecutor.us/strongsville-woman-sentenced-life-in-prison-crash-killed-two/), NBC News, Cleveland 19 and Netflix, checked on 25 November 2026. We've kept three things apart throughout: **what the court found**, **what Shirilla and her family say**, and **our own view**.

<Callout type="verdict">
**The quick answer.** This is not an open case. A judge heard the evidence and found, beyond reasonable doubt, that Mackenzie Shirilla deliberately drove into the wall, and an appeal court agreed: the accelerator was held to the floor, the brake was never touched, and someone moved the gear lever back into drive in the final seconds. Her claim of a medical blackout was considered and rejected. **Is *The Crash* worth watching?** Yes, for her prison interview and the way it shows a case being fought out on social media. But it's built to keep you guessing, and the court wasn't guessing. Watch it alongside the facts below.
</Callout>

## Who were Dominic Russo and Davion Flanagan?

The documentary's title and most of the coverage centre on Mackenzie Shirilla. It's worth starting with the two young men who died.

**Dominic Russo** was 20. He was Shirilla's boyfriend, and they had been together for several years. He was the youngest of seven children, and his family have been some of the most vocal voices in the case since. His sister Christine told Cleveland 19 the crash "has completely destroyed the entire family".

**Davion Flanagan** was 19, a friend of the couple who had recently left Strongsville High School. He had been adopted as a child by Jaime and Scott Flanagan, and his family set up a memorial scholarship for aspiring barbers in his name. "Justice was served for him today," his mother told reporters after the verdict.

Davion is easy to lose in this story. He had no part in the relationship at its centre; he was simply in the back seat.

## Who is Mackenzie Shirilla?

Mackenzie Shirilla was 17 and had just graduated from high school in Strongsville, a suburb south-west of Cleveland. She was active on social media, which became part of the case: prosecutors later showed videos of her at a concert and celebrating Halloween after the crash, arguing they showed a lack of remorse.

The appeal ruling records evidence of a turbulent relationship in the weeks before the crash: witnesses described fights in July 2022 in which Shirilla threatened and hit Russo, and some of this was backed up by video. One witness, a family friend who picked Russo up after an argument, testified that less than a month before the crash he heard her threaten to crash the car they were in. Friends and her mother testified that the couple seemed in love. The court heard both, and weighed them.

## What happened on 31 July 2022?

The night before, Shirilla, Russo and Flanagan went to a graduation party and then to a friend's house. They left in the early morning.

At about 5:30am, security cameras filmed Shirilla's 2018 Toyota Camry making what the investigating detective described as a controlled turn from Pearl Road onto Progress Drive, a road through a quiet industrial estate. Seconds later, another camera caught the car racing down the street. It crossed the junction with Alameda Drive, hit a kerb, went airborne and smashed into the brick wall of the Plidco building.

The car was found about 45 minutes later by a passer-by. Russo and Flanagan were pronounced dead at the scene. Shirilla was critically injured and taken to hospital.

## The investigation and the vehicle evidence

At first, police treated the crash as a terrible accident. The evidence that changed that came mainly from the car itself. The appeal court summarised what the trial heard:

- **The accelerator.** According to the crash reconstruction expert, the car's event data recorder showed the accelerator was fully pressed for the last 4.6 seconds before impact, and the throttle stayed at 100 per cent until the car left the ground.
- **No braking.** At no point in those seconds was the brake pressed.
- **The gear lever.** Someone in the car moved the gear lever into neutral, and it was then forced back into drive as the car kept going.
- **The steering.** The recorder logged a small right turn, a small left turn, then a hard yank of the wheel to the right about a second before the car hit the wall.
- **No defects.** A mechanical expert found nothing wrong with the accelerator, throttle, brakes or steering.
- **The route.** GPS data placed Shirilla's phone near Progress and Alameda three days earlier, on 28 July 2022, a road the trial judge said she didn't usually take.

Medical evidence mattered too. Doctors who examined Shirilla after the crash recorded no neurological, cardiovascular or muscular symptoms, and a blood alcohol test was negative. Police also found psilocybin mushrooms and a digital scale, which led to the drug possession and criminal tools convictions.

On 4 November 2022, Shirilla, by then 18, was charged. A juvenile court found there was not enough evidence to send an aggravated murder charge (which requires prior calculation) to adult court, but transferred the murder and other charges.

## The prosecution's case

Prosecutors argued that this was a deliberate act. Their case, as accepted by the court, rested on:

- **The data:** a foot held flat on the accelerator, no braking, and a car steered through a junction and into a wall.
- **The gear lever:** someone moved it back into drive. Prosecutors argued that was not the action of someone who had blacked out.
- **The route and timing:** an unfamiliar road she had been near days earlier, at 5:30am, when it would be empty.
- **The relationship:** the threats and fights in the weeks before, including the threat to crash a car with Russo in it.

## The defence's case

Shirilla's trial lawyer argued that she had no motive and no memory of the crash, and that something else might explain it:

- **A medical episode.** Her mother testified that Shirilla was diagnosed with postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) in 2017 and had had episodes since. The defence suggested POTS, a seizure or another medical emergency.
- **The relationship.** Friends and family described the couple as loving.
- **Other explanations.** Distraction or reckless driving rather than intent.

Under cross-examination, the trauma surgeon accepted that someone who had suffered a seizure or "mini-stroke" could have recovered by the time she was examined two hours later. The defence also pointed out that the data could not show why the steering wheel turned hard right.

## The verdict: what the court established

Shirilla chose a bench trial, so the verdict was given by a judge, Nancy Margaret Russo (no relation to Dominic). On 14 August 2023 she found Shirilla guilty of every count.

Her findings left no room for doubt. "This was not reckless driving. This was murder," she said, according to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/crime-courts/hell-wheels-ohio-teen-convicted-intentionally-crashing-car-100-mph-bui-rcna100194). She said the crash video "clearly shows the purpose and intent of the defendant", and that Shirilla went from "a responsible driver to literal hell on wheels". The appeal ruling records that the judge found Shirilla chose an obscure route she had visited days before, drove at a time when few people would be around, pressed the pedal to the floor and aimed the car at the wall, and that her actions were "controlled, methodical, deliberate, intentional, and purposeful". The judge said it could only be speculated whether Shirilla also meant to kill herself.

## The sentence

On 21 August 2023, Russo and Flanagan's families addressed the court. Prosecutors showed photos and videos of Shirilla after the crash and argued she had shown no remorse. Shirilla apologised to both families.

Calling the crash the result of a "selfish, intentional, and cruel decision", the judge sentenced her to 15 years to life for each murder, to run concurrently, and suspended her driving licence for life.

## The appeal: why the conviction was upheld

On 26 September 2024, a three-judge panel of the Eighth District Court of Appeals unanimously [upheld the convictions](https://www.supremecourt.ohio.gov/rod/docs/pdf/8/2024/2024-Ohio-4674.pdf). It's the most useful single document on this case, and it addresses the medical defence head-on.

The court accepted that a seizure or mini-stroke was possible in theory, but said other evidence made it "unreasonable". Shirilla had denied having any adverse medical condition when applying for a driving licence, both before and after the crash. And, the court said, "there was no evidence presented that any medical condition could have caused Shirilla to simultaneously lose the ability to take her foot off the accelerator or hit the brakes while also intentionally manipulating the gearshift." It also rejected distracted driving, because her foot never left the accelerator even as the car hit the kerb, went airborne and landed.

<Callout type="info">
**Court findings and claims.** That the crash was intentional is a finding of the trial court, upheld on appeal. That she had a medical episode, has no memory, or that Russo had grabbed the wheel on an earlier occasion are claims by Shirilla and her lawyers, which the courts have either rejected or not accepted as grounds to reopen the case.
</Callout>

## Where is Mackenzie Shirilla now?

Shirilla, now 22, is held at the Ohio Reformatory for Women in Marysville. [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/mackenzie-shirilla-ohio-teen-boyfriend-crash-case-rcna345021) reports that she will first be eligible for parole on 29 October 2037; Netflix says her first parole hearing is scheduled for September 2037.

Her appeals since the documentary have been about procedure as much as evidence:

- **A petition filed a day late.** Her post-conviction petition was filed on 24 October 2024, one day after the deadline, which her lawyers say was missed because 2024 was a leap year. The courts held they had no power to excuse the delay. The Ohio Supreme Court refused to hear the appeal in June 2026 and refused to reconsider in September.
- **Reopening the appeal.** In July 2026 new lawyers applied to reopen her direct appeal, arguing her earlier appellate lawyer was ineffective. The Eighth District refused on 12 August, finding the application 669 days late and unlikely to have changed the outcome. As [Cleveland 19 reported](https://www.cleveland19.com/2026/10/27/attorneys-mackenzie-shirilla-seek-reopen-appeal-deadly-strongsville-crash/) on 27 October, her lawyers have asked the Ohio Supreme Court to review that decision. They argue that text messages in which Russo allegedly admitted grabbing the wheel during an earlier incident should have been used, and they challenge the medical examiner's evidence. Prosecutors say the claims are late and without merit.

As of 25 November 2026 we have found no ruling on that request. The conviction and sentence stand.

### The families now

Christine Russo, Dominic's sister, has been outspoken since the documentary was released, telling Cleveland 19 that she believes Shirilla belongs in prison "for the remainder of her entire existence". Shirilla's parents, Steve and Natalie, maintain she is innocent and say she did not get a fair trial. After the film's release, Steve Shirilla was placed on administrative leave by Mary Queen of Peace School, where he worked, while it investigated allegations made on social media; he was later reported not to be returning.

## Netflix's The Crash: what the documentary shows

- **Title:** *The Crash*
- **Released:** 15 May 2026, worldwide, [including on Netflix UK](https://www.netflix.com/title/81941710)
- **Format:** a single documentary film, about an hour and a half
- **Director:** Gareth Johnson; **producer:** Angharad Scott
- **Netflix's synopsis:** "A teen slams her car into a building, killing her boyfriend and his friend. What seems like a tragic accident becomes a murder case in this documentary."

The film reconstructs the night and the investigation, then the trial, using the crash footage, police material and a great deal of Shirilla's own social media. It includes interviews with families, friends and investigators, and, most notably, Shirilla herself.

According to [Netflix's Tudum](https://www.netflix.com/tudum/articles/the-crash-where-is-mackenzie-shirilla-now), the crew had one hour with her in prison, and her lawyer was present throughout. She says she has no memory of that morning, that a medical emergency is "the most logical speculation", and: "I'm not saying I'm innocent. I was a driver of a tragedy, but I'm not a murderer."

### Is The Crash worth watching? Our verdict

**Yes, with a caveat.** Our verdict is based on the film's content as described by Netflix, the filmmakers and published reviews, set against the court record.

**What it does well.** Shirilla's interview is the reason to watch. It's the first time she has spoken at length, and seeing how she accounts for the crash, and what she won't address, tells you more than any number of TikTok clips. The film is also sharp on how this case was fought online: a defendant whose own social media became evidence, and an audience that turned a double killing into content. *The National*'s reviewer called it "taut and absorbing" and gave it 3.5 out of 5.

**Where it falls short.** The same review notes that it stretches ambiguity "for suspense even when the facts appear overwhelmingly clear". We think that's the central problem. The film's structure (accident or murder?) invites the viewer to treat as open a question the court answered on evidence, and an hour and a half isn't long enough to lay that evidence out properly. Dominic Russo's sister Christine, who saw it before release, told Cleveland 19 she was "shocked at the lies" in it, and that the friends interviewed were Shirilla's friends, not Dominic's.

**Our advice:** watch it for the interview and the social media story, then read the appeal court's summary of the evidence or listen to one of the evidence-led podcasts below.

### What the documentary leaves out or could explain better

- **The gear lever.** The detail the appeal court found most telling, that someone moved the car out of neutral and back into drive, is the strongest answer to the blackout theory. It deserves more weight than a medical theory the court rejected.
- **Why her medical claim failed.** POTS is presented as her explanation; the reasons the courts rejected it, including her licence applications and the medical examination, get less attention.
- **Davion Flanagan.** As in most coverage, he is overshadowed by the relationship at the centre of the story.
- **What came after.** The film was finished before the post-release appeals, the 32,000 messages released by police, and the arguments over the leap-year deadline.

## Cases like the Mackenzie Shirilla case

- **Karen Read.** A death involving a car, a contested account and a trial fought online. See our guide to [the Karen Read case and The Trials of Karen Read](/blog/karen-read-case-netflix-documentary-podcasts). Unlike Shirilla, Read was acquitted of murder.
- **Pamela Smart.** A young woman, a relationship and a killing that turned into a media spectacle. Our guide to [the Pamela Smart case and Netflix's A Deadly Affair](/blog/pamela-smart-case-netflix-documentary-podcasts) covers the case and the podcasts.
- **Michelle Carter.** A teenager convicted over her boyfriend's death, with her text messages at the heart of the case; covered in HBO's *I Love You, Now Die*.

For the debate about how true crime handles real victims, see our piece on [the ethics of true crime podcasting](/blog/ethics-of-true-crime-podcasting).

## The bottom line

Mackenzie Shirilla was convicted of murdering Dominic Russo and Davion Flanagan after a judge found she deliberately drove into a wall at about 100mph, and every court that has looked at the case since has left that verdict in place. *The Crash* is worth watching for her first interview and for what it shows about a case played out online, but it sometimes treats as an open question something the evidence settled. Watch it, then let the court record and the better podcasts fill in what it leaves out, and remember the two young men at the centre of it.
