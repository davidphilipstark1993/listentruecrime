Books on serial killers are a crowded shelf, and a lot of them are not worth your time. The genre has a long record of glamorising offenders, inventing detail and treating victims as scenery. The good ones do the opposite: they stay close to the evidence, take the victims seriously, and are honest about what cannot be known.

This guide picks a handful of books that clear that bar, and organises them by what they give you, not by how notorious the offender is. That is deliberate. If you want an investigator's view, a study of an offender, or a book that centres the people who were harmed, there is a different best answer for each. We pair each with a podcast on the site where a real connection exists, and we say plainly where we have none.

The cases in these books involve murder and sexual violence. We describe them factually and without graphic detail.

## If you want to understand how an offender hides

### The Stranger Beside Me — Ann Rule

Rule was a crime writer who had worked alongside Ted Bundy before he was identified as a suspect. The book follows the investigation, but its real subject is how someone who seems ordinary can go unseen by the people closest to him, including the author. It is long, it is dated in places, and it remains the most widely read account of the Bundy case.

Read it as a document of its time rather than a final word. **Pair it with** [The Bundy Tapes](/podcasts/the-bundy-tapes), a Netflix documentary series built on Bundy's own recorded conversations, which we rate as good if US true crime interests you.

## If you want the history of profiling

### Mindhunter — John E. Douglas and Mark Olshaker

Douglas was an FBI agent who, in the 1970s and 1980s, interviewed convicted serial killers to understand how they thought, and helped build the methods the Bureau's Behavioral Science Unit became known for. The book is his account of that work, and it was the basis for the Netflix series of the same name.

It is a memoir, and Douglas is the hero of his own story. Criminal profiling has since faced serious scrutiny from researchers who question how reliable it is. That does not make the book unreadable. It makes it a first-hand record of how the FBI came to think about offenders, which is a different thing from a guide to how offenders actually behave.

## If you want a tightly structured single case

### American Predator — Maureen Callahan

Callahan's book follows Israel Keyes, the serial killer caught in 2012 after the murder of Samantha Koenig. What sets it apart is the investigation that followed, and how little the investigators understood about him until he was in custody.

It is linear, controlled and restrained, which makes it one of the better books on this list for audio. **Pair it with** the [American Predator podcast](/podcasts/american-predator), a scripted single-narrator series on the same case that we rate **Worth a listen**, with a binge factor of 7. The two are close in tone, and either is a good way into the other.

## If you want the journalists' account

### Bind, Torture, Kill — Roy Wenzl, Tim Potter, L. Kelly and Hurst Laviana

This is the BTK case, by the Wichita Eagle journalists who covered it. It is a newsroom book, written by the people who covered the case as it unfolded. That vantage point is rare among offender books, and it keeps the community, not just the offender, in the frame.

**Pair it with** [Monster: BTK](/podcasts/monster-btk), an 8 to 10 episode series that examines the case of a man who killed 10 people over 30 years while serving as a church council president, rated by us as a **Must listen**, with a binge factor of 7.

## If you want the victims at the centre

Some of the best writing on this subject is the writing that moves attention away from the offender. Two books are worth knowing about here, even though we have not linked them.

**Lost Girls: An American Mystery** by Robert Kolker is about the women whose bodies were found near Gilgo Beach, Long Island, and the families who pushed for answers. It is a book about who the women were and how their disappearances were treated, not about the person responsible. For the case itself, see our [Gilgo Beach case page](/cases/gilgo-beach-long-island-serial-killer), and for podcasts, [LISK: Long Island Serial Killer](/podcasts/lisk-long-island-serial-killer), which we rate good if you want dedicated, ongoing coverage of the case, with a binge factor of 8.

**The Five** by Hallie Rubenhold does the same for the women killed in Whitechapel in 1888, and is covered in our [British true crime books and audiobooks](/blog/best-british-true-crime-books-and-audiobooks) guide.

## If you want the family's perspective

**Shattered Silence** by Melissa G. Moore is a memoir of growing up as the daughter of Keith Hunter Jesperson, who became known as the Happy Face Killer. It is a very different book from the others here, because it is about what it is to live with that knowledge, and it asks a question most serial killer books do not: what happens to the people left behind on the offender's side.

## If you want a historical puzzle, with caution

**The Man from the Train** by Bill James and Rachel McCarthy James attempts to connect the 1912 Villisca axe murders in Iowa to a series of similar family killings across the US, and to name a single suspect. It is a fascinating piece of detective work and also a speculative one, and it is best read as an argument rather than a conclusion. We mention it for the reader who wants something more analytical, and flag it so no one mistakes it for settled history.

## How to pace this reading

This is heavy material, and it is worth pacing. Many readers find it helps to alternate: a book about an offender, then something lighter, or a book that keeps the victims in view. Check the content of a book before you start it, and be especially careful with audiobooks, because you cannot skim past a passage the way you can on a page. If a book is not working for you, stopping is a perfectly good decision. Nothing about the genre requires you to finish what you start.

## A word on how to read this genre

Two habits make a real difference. First, check when the book was written and what has been learned since: many of the classics predate DNA evidence, and some of their conclusions have not held up. Second, notice whose story the book is telling. The strongest books in this genre keep the people who were harmed in view, and the weakest turn the offender into a character you are supposed to find fascinating.
