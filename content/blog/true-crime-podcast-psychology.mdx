More than half of all podcast listeners consume true crime content regularly. In the UK, it's consistently the most popular podcast genre. In the US, the top true crime podcasts attract tens of millions of listeners.

We're clearly obsessed. But why?

The question is worth asking seriously — both because the psychology behind the genre is genuinely interesting, and because understanding it makes you a better, more reflective consumer of the content.

## The Numbers First

True crime isn't a niche. It's mainstream entertainment:

- Crime Junkie has approximately 30 million downloads per month
- My Favorite Murder has over 600 million downloads since its 2016 launch
- Serial attracted over 175 million downloads for its first season alone
- In a typical week, the top 20 podcasts in the UK include between 5 and 10 true crime shows

The demographic skew is significant: studies consistently find that women comprise 70-80% of true crime podcast audiences. The reasons for this are themselves revealing.

## The Psychological Mechanisms

### Curiosity About Extreme Human Behaviour

Human beings are naturally curious about outlier behaviour — about the edge cases of what humans are capable of. Serial killers, mass murderers, and perpetrators of spectacular frauds represent the extreme end of human capacity for harm.

This curiosity is adaptive: understanding what humans can do allows us to recognise warning signs, protect ourselves and others, and maintain accurate models of how dangerous the world is. It's not morbid fascination — it's how our threat-recognition systems learn.

### The 'Scared But Safe' Emotional Experience

Horror films, true crime, and dark fiction all produce a specific emotional experience: the feeling of fear without genuine danger. Psychological research — including work by Mathias Clasen on horror consumption — suggests this is pleasurable partly because it allows emotional arousal and release in a controlled context, and partly because surviving the emotional experience (even fictionally) produces a mild positive feeling.

True crime offers a particularly potent version of this: the fear is grounded in reality, which makes it more intense, but the listener is physically safe, which makes it tolerable.

Women access this experience through true crime specifically, researchers suggest, because the crimes most represented in the genre — domestic violence, stalking, sexual crime — are also the crimes that women face a disproportionate risk of experiencing. The 'scared but safe' framing may double as functional threat preparation.

### The Justice Paradox

True crime stories have a narrative structure: crime, investigation, resolution (or failure of resolution). In the best cases, justice is served — a conviction, an exoneration, a cold case solved. In the worst, it isn't — and this creates a specific emotional response: frustration that functions as engagement.

Research suggests that people are particularly drawn to narratives with unresolved injustice — which explains why cold cases and wrongful conviction stories generate such intense listener investment. We want resolution. When we don't get it, we come back.

Podcasts like Your Own Backyard, which followed an investigation in real time until a conviction was secured, may work partly because they provide the resolution that conventional cold case coverage can't.

### The Understanding Gap

Most of us have never encountered violent crime. We have no direct experience of police investigations, criminal trials, or the forensic process. True crime podcasts are genuinely educational in the sense that they give listeners a model of how these systems work — a model that is often more accurate (or at least more complex) than what popular TV crime drama provides.

This is particularly true for investigative podcasts that follow real investigations. Serial gave millions of people a more sophisticated understanding of how memory, evidence, and legal procedure interact than any crime drama could. Understanding that eyewitness testimony is unreliable, that confessions can be coerced, and that prosecutors sometimes suppress evidence isn't just trivia — it's civic education.

### Empathy and Advocacy

A significant portion of true crime listeners are motivated primarily by empathy — a desire to bear witness to victims who might otherwise be forgotten, and to advocate for justice in cases that have been neglected.

This is particularly visible in missing persons communities, where listeners often maintain years-long investment in specific cases and contribute tips, research, and community pressure that can have real investigative consequences.

My Favorite Murder explicitly frames itself as a community of people who care about victims: the show's famous exhortation to its listeners is "Stay Sexy and Don't Get Murdered" — which is simultaneously a joke and a genuine form of care about each other's safety.

## The Ethical Complications

The motivations above are largely benign. But true crime consumption raises ethical questions that serious fans should sit with:

**Do victims' families consent?**

The families of murder victims have not consented to having their relatives' deaths become entertainment. Many actively oppose the podcast treatment of their relatives. The ethical tension here isn't resolved by good intentions — it requires ongoing attention to how creators handle the people closest to the crimes they cover.

**Does true crime distort our understanding of crime?**

The cases that generate true crime podcasts are not representative of crime. They tend to involve white victims more than victims of colour, attractive young women more than elderly men, dramatic narratives more than ordinary domestic violence. Consuming only true crime as a window onto crime produces a skewed picture.

**Does consumption reward exploitation?**

Some true crime podcasts handle material irresponsibly — exploiting victims' families, sensationalising violence, prioritising emotional impact over accuracy. Listening to them rewards that behaviour commercially. The choice of which podcasts to support matters.

## What the Best Podcasts Get Right

The true crime podcasts that navigate this territory best share several characteristics:

**Victim-centred storytelling.** Victims are introduced as people before they become victims. Their lives, relationships, and humanity matter as much as the crime.

**Honest uncertainty.** Good true crime acknowledges what isn't known rather than filling gaps with plausible-sounding narrative.

**Explicit ethics.** The best podcasters — Karen Kilgariff and Georgia Hardstark at My Favorite Murder, Connie Walker at Missing and Murdered — are explicit about why they're making this content and what obligations they feel toward the people whose stories they're telling.

**Proportionality.** The amount of attention given to gory details versus the victim's humanity is a reliable indicator of a podcast's ethics.

## The Bottom Line

True crime obsession isn't pathological. It's an engagement with real human events, real institutional failures, and real questions about justice, psychology, and human behaviour. The mechanisms driving it — threat learning, justice seeking, empathy, curiosity — are all recognisable aspects of normal psychology.

What you do with that engagement matters. Support good investigative journalism. Donate to organisations like the Innocence Project. Apply critical thinking to the forensic evidence you're presented with. And pay attention to how the podcasts you consume treat the people at the centre of their stories.
