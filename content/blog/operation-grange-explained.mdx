When Portugal's attorney general archived the original investigation into Madeleine McCann's disappearance in July 2008, the case could easily have faded into the long list of unsolved European missing-person files, revisited only by anniversary journalism. That it didn't is largely down to one thing: Operation Grange, the Metropolitan Police's dedicated investigation, which has now run for the better part of fifteen years and, as of 2026, remains funded and active.

## From Political Pressure to a Formal Review

Operation Grange didn't begin as a criminal investigation in its own right. In May 2011, then-Home Secretary Theresa May, backed by Prime Minister David Cameron, directed Scotland Yard to review the existing Portuguese investigation files, following sustained lobbying from [Kate and Gerry McCann's campaign](/blog/kate-and-gerry-mccann-19-year-fight). DCI Andy Redwood led the review, reporting to Detective Chief Superintendent Hamish Campbell, with an initial team modest by the standards of a homicide inquiry but substantial for what was, at that point, officially just a document review.

The team spent its first two years working through tens of thousands of translated documents from the original Portuguese file and cataloguing more than 8,000 reported sightings of Madeleine from around the world — the overwhelming majority of which led nowhere.

## From Review to Full Investigation

In July 2013, the review formally became Operation Grange, a full Metropolitan Police investigation in its own right. Redwood publicly framed the disappearance as most likely "a criminal act by a stranger" — either a planned abduction or an opportunist burglary that went wrong when Madeleine woke — a framing that has, with variations, guided the investigation's priorities ever since.

The same year produced one of the operation's most publicly visible moments: a special edition of BBC's *Crimewatch*, broadcast on 14 October 2013, built around two new e-fit images of a man witnesses had described near the McCanns' apartment. Around seven million people watched. The programme also delivered the announcement, days later, that the man originally seen by witness Jane Tanner at 9.15pm had been identified as an innocent British holidaymaker collecting his own daughter — a finding that effectively closed off one of the case's oldest leads.

## Scaling Down: 2015 Onwards

Investigations of this scale rarely sustain their initial staffing indefinitely. By February 2015, with the most resource-intensive review phase essentially complete, the Metropolitan Police reduced the team from 29 officers at its peak to just four working full time. DCI Nicola Wall took over as senior investigating officer following Redwood's retirement in 2014.

From 2015 onward, Operation Grange has existed largely through a series of funding extensions from the Home Office, each requiring fresh justification. By 2024, funding decisions had settled into an annual rhythm, supporting a part-time team of three or four people — a fraction of the original 29-strong unit, but enough to keep the investigation formally active. Documented lifetime costs have run to roughly £13 million.

<Callout type="info">
Operation Grange's annual cost has fallen by well over 90 percent from its early-2010s peak of several million pounds a year to a low six-figure sum today, run by a skeleton team rather than the nearly thirty officers involved at its height. Critics have periodically questioned whether an investigation costing this much over its lifetime is proportionate for a single case; supporters point to the eventual identification of a suspect as evidence that sustained investigative work can still produce leads many years on.
</Callout>

## What Operation Grange Has Ruled Out

Much of Operation Grange's real work, particularly in its first decade, has involved elimination rather than confirmation — narrowing the field by ruling people out rather than building a case against one individual. The team formally cleared the man identified from Jane Tanner's 2007 sighting in 2013. A group of men suspected of involvement in a series of local burglaries, investigated intensively around the case's ten-year anniversary in 2017, were also eventually ruled out through mobile phone data and confirmed movements on the night in question.

This elimination work rarely made headlines the way a new suspect or a new search does, but it's arguably where a large proportion of the operation's budget and staff time has actually gone.

## A New Investigative Focus: Brueckner

Operation Grange's centre of gravity shifted meaningfully in 2020, when German police identified [Christian Brueckner](/blog/who-is-christian-brueckner-madeleine-mccann) as their suspect, and again in 2022, when Portuguese prosecutors formally made him an arguido. Much of the operation's recent activity — including a substantial handover of German case files to British detectives in 2026 — has been organised around building a case connected to Brueckner specifically, a considerably narrower focus than the broad review the operation began as in 2011.

## 2026: Funding Secured, Investigation Still Active

As of 2026, Operation Grange remains funded and formally active. The investigation's current focus centres heavily on the legal process around Brueckner in Germany, where prosecutors are seeking to overturn his October 2024 acquittal on unrelated charges and secure a retrial — a process that could shape what evidence becomes available to support any future British case. Whatever its critics say about cost, Operation Grange's basic historical function isn't seriously in dispute: without it, the investigation into Madeleine's disappearance would very likely have ended, in practical terms, in the summer of 2008.
