**Adnan Syed was not exonerated. He has been free since September 2022, but his 2000 conviction for the murder of Hae Min Lee stands.** In March 2025 a Baltimore judge cut his sentence to the roughly 23 years he had already served, under a Maryland law for people convicted of crimes committed as children. He is on five years of supervised probation and still says he is innocent.

That is a long way from how Serial left things in 2014, and from the "exonerated" headlines of 2022. This page sets out what happened, in order, with the court rulings and reporting I checked. As of 23 November 2026, I found no reported court ruling in the case since the March 2025 resentencing.

## Where the case stands now

| | Status (23 November 2026) |
|---|---|

| **Sentence** | Originally life plus 30 years; reduced in March 2025 to time served |
| **In prison?** | No. Out since 19 September 2022 |
| **Probation** | Five years, supervised, from March 2025 |
| **Exonerated?** | No |
| **Anyone else charged?** | No |

Hae Min Lee was 18 and a senior at Woodlawn High School in Baltimore County when she disappeared on 13 January 1999. Her body was found in Leakin Park, in west Baltimore, on 9 February. Syed, her former boyfriend, was 17 when he was arrested at the end of that month.

## What Serial revealed, and what it didn't resolve

Sarah Koenig's [Serial](/podcasts/serial) didn't set out to free anyone, and it didn't claim to know who killed Hae Min Lee. Across 12 episodes it showed how thin parts of the case were. The State's star witness, Jay Wilds, had changed his account of the day several times. The cell phone records that seemed to back him up were less precise than the jury may have assumed. And a classmate, Asia McClain, said she had seen Syed in the public library at around the time the State said Lee was killed, but his trial lawyer never contacted her.

What Serial couldn't do was settle any of it. Koenig ended the season saying that, as a juror, she would have voted to acquit, but that she didn't know whether he did it. The legal fight that followed was about those same questions: whether his defence had failed him, and whether the jury had heard everything it should have.

If you want the reporting itself, start with [Serial Season 1](/podcasts/serial). For a much deeper and openly pro-defence look at the evidence, [Undisclosed](/podcasts/undisclosed) was made by three lawyers, including Rabia Chaudry, the family friend who first took the case to Koenig.

## 2015–2019: a new trial, then the conviction is reinstated

Serial's attention helped Syed's appeal lawyer, Justin Brown, reopen the case in 2015. In June 2016, Judge Martin Welch ordered a new trial. He found that Syed's trial lawyer, Cristina Gutierrez, "rendered ineffective assistance when she failed to cross-examine the state's expert regarding the reliability of cell tower location evidence". The records came with a fax cover sheet warning that incoming calls weren't reliable for location. Welch rejected the alibi claim about Asia McClain.

In March 2018 the Court of Special Appeals upheld the new trial, 2-1, but on the McClain ground instead: failing to contact her, the majority said, could "have raised a reasonable doubt in the mind of at least one juror".

That ruling didn't last. On 8 March 2019, Maryland's highest court reversed it, 4-3, holding that "there is not a significant or substantial possibility that the verdict would have been different". The cell tower claim also failed. In November 2019 the US Supreme Court declined to take the case. His conviction and sentence were back where they had been in 2000.

## 2022: vacated, released and the charges dropped

In September 2022, Baltimore prosecutors under State's Attorney Marilyn Mosby asked a judge to vacate the conviction themselves. Their motion said a year-long review had "uncovered Brady violations and new information, all concerning the possible involvement of two alternate suspects". In other words, they said prosecutors had withheld evidence that could have helped Syed. The motion also raised doubts about the cell phone evidence. It was explicit that the State was not then asserting that Syed was innocent, only that it had lost confidence in the conviction.

On 19 September 2022 Judge Melissa Phinn granted the motion, and Syed walked out of court after 23 years in prison. On 11 October, Mosby dropped the charges, telling reporters that new DNA testing had excluded him. That is the moment most people remember as his exoneration.

## 2023–2024: the conviction is reinstated

Hae Min Lee's brother, Young Lee, had been told about the September hearing less than one business day beforehand. He asked for a delay so he could travel from California, was refused, and watched by video. He appealed.

On 28 March 2023 the Appellate Court of Maryland (as the Court of Special Appeals had been renamed in December 2022) ruled 2-1 that his rights as a crime victim's representative had been violated. It reinstated the conviction, held that the dropping of charges had no legal effect, and ordered a new, properly run hearing on the motion to vacate.

On 30 August 2024 the Supreme Court of Maryland upheld that ruling, 4-3. The majority said that in trying to put right what they saw as an injustice to Syed, the prosecutor and the court had worked an injustice against Young Lee. It also held that victims' representatives have a right to be heard on the merits of a motion to vacate. Syed was allowed to stay free while the case went back to the circuit court.

Neither court decided whether Syed killed Hae Min Lee. Both rulings were about process.

## 2025: the motion withdrawn, and a reduced sentence

By then Baltimore had a new State's Attorney, Ivan Bates. His office reviewed the 2022 motion, not the whole murder case, and on 25 February 2025 withdrew it. Its written summary says the motion's claims were "not supported by fact or law". It argued that the two handwritten notes at the heart of the motion did not point to other suspects, that both men were known to the defence before trial, and that the notes were probably disclosed at the time. It also dismissed the DNA, which came from the soles of a pair of shoes found in Lee's car. The summary says the office "does not make any judgment regarding Mr. Syed's innocence or guilt". Syed's lawyer, Erica Suter, said Bates "got it wrong".

Withdrawing the motion left the 2000 conviction in place, with no new hearing to come. What kept Syed out of prison was a separate application under Maryland's Juvenile Restoration Act. That law lets people who were under 18 at the time of their crime, and have served at least 20 years, ask a court to reduce their sentence. Bates's office supported it.

On 6 March 2025, Judge Jennifer Schiffer granted it. She found Syed was not a danger to the public and that the interests of justice would be better served by a reduced sentence. On 14 March she formally resentenced him to time served, with five years of supervised probation. She refused his request for unsupervised probation but allowed him to travel to Washington, D.C. and Virginia without asking his probation agent first. The original sentence remains suspended over him.

## Was Adnan Syed exonerated?

No, and the word is the main source of confusion about this case.

In ordinary use, an exoneration means a conviction has been wiped out and the person cleared. That usually happens through an acquittal at a retrial, the charges being dismissed for good, or a pardon based on innocence. For a few months in late 2022, Syed's case looked like that: the conviction was vacated, the charges were dropped, and Mosby called it a wrongful conviction. Many outlets reported it as an exoneration.

The appeals courts then undid both steps. The vacatur was reinstated, and the dropped charges were treated as having no legal effect. Since the 2025 withdrawal and resentencing, the legal position is clear:

- **He is convicted.** The 2000 jury verdict stands, and no court has found him innocent.
- **He is free.** That is because his sentence was reduced, not because his conviction was overturned.
- **The prosecutor's view has changed.** The 2022 office said it had lost confidence in the conviction; the current office says the motion behind that claim was unfounded and that it believes in the jury's verdict.

Syed has always said he did not kill Hae Min Lee. After the 2024 ruling, Suter said the team would "work tirelessly to clear his name". As of November 2026 I found no reported filing that has done so.

## What remains disputed

Courts have ruled on procedure. They have not settled these factual questions, and people who know the case well still disagree:

- **Jay Wilds's testimony.** He was the State's central witness, and his account changed over time.
- **The cell phone evidence.** The defence says the fax cover sheet's warning undermined it. Bates's office says the State's expert relied on his own drive tests, not on those records.
- **Asia McClain's alibi.** Two courts found the failure to contact her mattered; Maryland's highest court found it would not have changed the verdict.
- **The "alternative suspects".** The 2022 motion said evidence about two men was withheld; the 2025 review says it wasn't, and that they were known to the defence. Neither man has been charged.
- **The DNA.** Mosby said it excluded Syed. Bates says it came from shoes never shown to be Lee's and proves nothing about her murder.

## Hae Min Lee and her family

It is easy, after a decade of podcasts and documentaries, to lose sight of the fact that this case is about the killing of an 18-year-old. Her family did not take part in Serial, and their appeal was what changed the case in 2023. Their lawyer, David Sanford, said in 2023: "We're not on a campaign to have Adnan Syed put back in jail. This is about respecting victims and their representatives." After the March 2025 resentencing he said it "brings to a close the long saga of Adnan Syed".

## Where Adnan Syed is now

Syed, now in his mid-40s, works at Georgetown University's Prisons and Justice Initiative, which hired him after his release, and has family in Virginia. In September 2025, HBO added *Part Five: The Tree Grew* to Amy Berg's documentary series *The Case Against Adnan Syed*, following him after his release.
