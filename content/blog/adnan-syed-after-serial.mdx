Most people who listened to Serial in 2014 know how the podcast itself ended: inconclusively, with Sarah Koenig unable to say for certain whether Adnan Syed killed Hae Min Lee. Fewer people followed what happened after — and the actual ending, which took another eleven years to arrive, is a lot stranger than "he was exonerated," which is the version a lot of people seem to have absorbed by osmosis. It isn't quite that. Here's what actually happened, in order.

## 2016–2019: The appeals

Serial's biggest legal contribution wasn't really the podcast itself — it was Syed's attorney, Justin Brown, using the show's renewed public attention to push a post-conviction appeal focused on Syed's trial lawyer's failure to investigate a potential alibi witness, Asia McClain. A Baltimore judge granted Syed a new trial in 2016. Maryland's Court of Special Appeals upheld that decision in 2018. Then, in March 2019, Maryland's highest court reversed it, reinstating the original conviction. For three years, this looked like the end of the road.

## September 2022: The vacatur

It wasn't. In September 2022, Baltimore prosecutors — after a year-long joint investigation with Syed's defense team — asked a judge to vacate the conviction entirely, citing newly disclosed evidence about two alternative suspects, including a man named Ronald Lee Moore, that had never been turned over to Syed's original defense. The judge agreed. Syed walked out of the courthouse a free man after 23 years in prison. Weeks later, without enough evidence to retry him, prosecutors formally dropped the charges.

This is the point where most casual followers of the story stopped paying attention, and reasonably concluded the case was over.

## March 2023–2024: The reversal

It wasn't over. Hae Min Lee's brother, Young Lee, appealed the vacatur — not on the merits of Syed's innocence or guilt, but on a procedural ground: Maryland law entitles crime victims' families to adequate notice before a hearing like the one that freed Syed, and Young Lee argued he hadn't gotten it. In March 2023, Maryland's Court of Special Appeals agreed and reinstated Syed's conviction. Maryland's Supreme Court upheld that reinstatement in August 2024. Syed remained free throughout — the ruling was about the process, not about sending him back to prison immediately — but his conviction was, on paper, restored.

## February–March 2025: The actual ending

Baltimore elected a new State's Attorney, Ivan Bates, who ordered a fresh review of the entire case file — including new DNA testing on evidence from the crime scene. On 25 February 2025, Bates's office withdrew the original motion to vacate altogether, effectively declining to pursue exoneration any further. Then, on 6 March 2025, a Maryland judge granted a separate motion: a sentence reduction to time served under the state's Juvenile Restoration Act, which allows reduced sentences for people convicted of crimes committed before they turned 18. Syed was 17 when Hae Min Lee was killed.

The result is the actual, current state of the case: Adnan Syed is free, living in the Washington, D.C. area, working at Georgetown University's Prisons and Justice Initiative — and still, legally, a convicted murderer, on five years of supervised probation. Not exonerated. Not returned to prison. Something in between that Serial's original 12 episodes never really had a category for.

## What this means for the case itself

None of this changes what Serial actually was: investigative journalism that surfaced real, serious problems with the original trial — an unreliable key witness, cell tower evidence a state expert later admitted was unreliable, an alibi witness who was never called. What it does mean is that "the podcast that got an innocent man out of prison" was never quite accurate, and "exonerated" still isn't. Hae Min Lee's murder remains, on the official record, unsolved. Her family has said, in the years since, that the endless re-litigation of the case has done them no favours either way — win or lose, it means opening the same wound again.
