An investigative podcast is a documentary series where a reporter goes and finds things out: tracks down witnesses, obtains court and police records, and tests the official account of what happened. That is what separates it from the much larger number of true crime shows that retell a case from news coverage, however well.

The twelve series below all meet that test. In each one the reporting itself is the story, and in several it changed the case: a conviction overturned, a cold case charged, a missing child's fate finally explained. We chose them for the quality of the reporting first, then for how well they hold up as listening. Where a show is harrowing, we say so.

If you just want a good story, our [best true crime podcasts](/best-true-crime-podcasts) list covers every style. If you are new to the genre, start with our [guide for beginners](/blog/best-true-crime-podcasts-for-beginners).

## How to tell an investigative podcast from a retelling

Four things separate the two.

- **New voices.** Investigative series interview people who have not spoken publicly before. If every quote has already appeared in a newspaper, it is a retelling.
- **Documents.** Court transcripts, police files, records requests. Good investigations tell you where each fact came from.
- **Honest uncertainty.** Real reporting often ends without a neat answer. The best hosts say so rather than inventing a resolution.
- **Accountability.** The reporting is aimed at someone: a prosecutor, an institution or a suspect who was never charged.

None of this makes a retelling worthless. A show like [Casefile](/podcasts/casefile-true-crime) researches carefully and tells cases very well. It just isn't doing the same job as the series below.

## The landmark investigations

### In the Dark

**Season 2 is the best investigative podcast we have heard.** APM Reports moved a team to Winona, Mississippi, to examine how Curtis Flowers came to be tried six times for the same 1996 quadruple murder by the same prosecutor. The reporters interviewed witnesses again, found that key testimony had shifted, and documented how Black jurors had been repeatedly struck from his juries. In 2019 the US Supreme Court overturned his conviction on the jury discrimination issue, and the charges were dropped in 2020.

Season 1 looks at the 1989 abduction of Jacob Wetterling and how the investigation failed. Season 3, made with The New Yorker, investigates the 2005 killings of Iraqi civilians by US Marines in Haditha.

**Best for:** listeners who want to see how a justice system can fail, step by step.

### Serial

Season 1 (2014) is the series that made investigative podcasting mainstream. Sarah Koenig spent a year re-examining the evidence against Adnan Syed, convicted of murdering his ex-girlfriend Hae Min Lee in 1999. It doesn't reach a verdict, and its refusal to pretend otherwise is a large part of why it works.

The case has moved on since. Syed's conviction was vacated in 2022, reinstated on appeal, and in 2025 his sentence was reduced to time served, though the conviction itself stands. Our article on [what happened to Adnan Syed after Serial](/blog/adnan-syed-after-serial) explains the legal steps. Season 3, a year inside a Cleveland courthouse, is the best of the later seasons.

**Best for:** first-time listeners to investigative audio. If you liked it, see [podcasts like Serial](/blog/podcasts-like-serial).

### The Teacher's Pet

Hedley Thomas of The Australian investigated the 1982 disappearance of Lynette Dawson from Sydney's northern beaches. The series gathered testimony from former students and family members, and its audience put the case back in front of the authorities. Her husband, Chris Dawson, was charged in 2018 and convicted of her murder in 2022. The podcast was withdrawn in Australia after he was charged, so that it could not affect the trial, which tells you how closely it touched a live case.

**Best for:** anyone who wants a complete story with a resolution.

### Your Own Backyard

Chris Lambert, a musician with no background in journalism, started reporting on the 1996 disappearance of Cal Poly student Kristin Smart in 2019. His interviews brought new witnesses forward. Paul Flores was arrested in 2021 and convicted of her murder in 2022, and the San Luis Obispo County Sheriff's Office credited the podcast with helping. It shows what persistent local reporting can do.

**Best for:** listeners interested in cold cases. Our list of [podcasts that solved real crimes](/blog/true-crime-podcasts-solved-real-crimes) covers it alongside others.

## Investigations into institutions

### Believed

Michigan Radio's series on Larry Nassar, the USA Gymnastics and Michigan State University doctor, focuses less on Nassar than on why the people around him did not act. Kate Wells and Lindsey Smith interviewed survivors, parents and the detective who built the case. It is careful with survivors' accounts and avoids graphic detail, but the subject is child sexual abuse.

**Best for:** understanding how institutions protect abusers.

### Bone Valley

Gilbert King, who won a Pulitzer Prize for a book about a Florida injustice, spent years on the case of Leo Schofield, convicted of murdering his wife Michelle in 1987. Fingerprints from the crime scene were later matched to another man, Jeremy Scott, who has since confessed several times. Schofield was released on parole in 2024, but his conviction stands and prosecutors still say he killed his wife. It is a slow, thorough look at how hard it is to undo a conviction.

**Best for:** listeners who want a wrongful-conviction series. Our [wrongful conviction podcasts guide](/blog/wrongful-conviction-podcasts) has more.

### Catch and Kill: The Podcast Tapes

Ronan Farrow's companion to his book about the Harvey Weinstein investigation. It uses recordings and interviews from his reporting, including conversations with sources and with the private investigators hired to follow him. It is closer to a journalist's diary than a crime narrative, which makes it a good insight into how investigations are actually done.

**Best for:** anyone interested in the reporting process itself.

## Missing people, unnamed victims and old crimes

### Missing and Murdered: Finding Cleo

Connie Walker's second season of Missing and Murdered follows the family of Cleo Semaganis Nicotine, a Cree girl taken from her family in Saskatchewan during the Sixties Scoop, when thousands of Indigenous children were removed and adopted out. Her siblings had grown up believing she was murdered. Walker's reporting found out what really happened to her. It won best serialised story at the 2018 Third Coast International Audio Festival and is told with real care for the family. Walker's later series, *Stolen: Surviving St. Michael's*, won a Pulitzer Prize and a Peabody.

**Best for:** listeners who want an investigation with historical weight.

### Bear Brook

In 1985 and 2000, the remains of four people were found in barrels in a New Hampshire state park. For decades no one knew who they were. Jason Moon of NHPR follows the investigators and genetic genealogists who identified them and the man responsible. The series explains the science clearly, and our [genetic genealogy explainer](/blog/genetic-genealogy-cold-cases) covers the technique in more depth.

**Best for:** listeners interested in forensic science.

### White Lies

Two Alabama-born journalists reinvestigate the 1965 killing of the Rev. James Reeb, a white minister attacked in Selma during the voting rights campaign. Three men were acquitted in 1965. Chip Brantley and Andrew Beck Grace found witnesses who had never spoken, and named a fourth man who was there and never charged. It is as much about how a town kept a secret for more than 50 years as about the crime.

**Best for:** listeners who like history and journalism together.

## Global investigations

### The Missing Cryptoqueen

The BBC's Jamie Bartlett investigated OneCoin, the cryptocurrency scheme that took about $4 billion from investors worldwide, and its founder Ruja Ignatova, who vanished in 2017 and is on the FBI's Most Wanted list. It is pacy, often darkly funny, and the reporting reaches victims in several countries. More fraud stories are in our [fraud and scam podcasts guide](/blog/best-fraud-scam-podcasts).

**Best for:** listeners who prefer financial crime to violent crime.

### Hunting Warhead

A co-production between CBC Podcasts and the Norwegian newspaper VG. VG journalist Håkon Høydal and CBC host Daemon Fairless follow the police and reporters who brought down one of the largest child abuse sites on the dark web and identified its Canadian administrator. Nothing graphic is described, but the subject is extremely difficult and many listeners will choose to skip it.

**Best for:** listeners who want to understand online child exploitation and how it is policed. Not a casual listen.

## Also worth your time

- **[Death in Ice Valley](/podcasts/death-in-ice-valley)** (BBC World Service and NRK): an attempt to identify a woman found dead near Bergen in 1970. More mystery than accountability, so it is also on our [murder mystery list](/blog/best-murder-mystery-podcasts).
- **[The Dropout](/podcasts/the-dropout)** (ABC News): Rebecca Jarvis on Elizabeth Holmes and Theranos, with interviews with former employees and investors.
- **[Dr. Death](/podcasts/dr-death)** (Wondery): Laura Beil, an experienced medical journalist, on the Dallas neurosurgeon Christopher Duntsch and the hospitals that let him keep operating.
- **[Undisclosed](/podcasts/undisclosed)**: lawyers Rabia Chaudry, Susan Simpson and Colin Miller go through the evidence in contested convictions. It is legal analysis rather than field reporting, but it is detailed and well sourced.

## Which investigative podcast should you start with?

- **The best overall:** In the Dark, Season 2.
- **A complete story in a weekend:** The Teacher's Pet or Believed.
- **A case changed by the podcast:** Your Own Backyard.
- **Something other than murder:** The Missing Cryptoqueen or Catch and Kill.
- **History and civil rights:** White Lies.
