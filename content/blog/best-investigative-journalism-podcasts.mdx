There's a meaningful distinction between a podcast that tells you about a crime and one that actively investigates it. The first is entertainment. The second is journalism — and at its best, it's some of the most important journalism produced anywhere, in any medium.

This guide covers the podcasts that have done the latter. These are shows that broke new information, freed innocent people, contributed to arrests, or changed policy. In a genre full of retold stories and sensationalised content, these are the ones that genuinely matter.

## Serial: The Podcast That Started Everything

It's impossible to discuss investigative true crime podcasting without beginning here. **Serial Season 1** — Sarah Koenig's investigation into the 1999 conviction of Adnan Syed for the murder of Hae Min Lee — didn't just create a cultural moment. It established that audio journalism could reach a mass audience, that podcasting was a serious medium, and that one show could shift a country's understanding of how the criminal justice system works.

Syed's conviction was subsequently vacated in 2022 (though later reinstated after a complication around victim notification rights — the legal situation remains complex). Whatever you think of Syed's guilt or innocence, Serial proved that a podcast could apply enough pressure and public scrutiny to move a legal case that had been considered closed.

**Rating: 10/10 for investigative impact and production quality**

## Your Own Backyard: The Podcast That Solved a Murder

We've covered this elsewhere, but it bears repeating: **Your Own Backyard** by Chris Lambert is the most consequential cold case podcast ever made.

Lambert investigated the 1996 disappearance of Kristin Smart for years, building a case through witness interviews, document analysis, and sustained public pressure that contributed directly to the 2021 arrest of Paul Flores — and his 2022 murder conviction, 25 years after Kristin disappeared.

This is not a podcast that retold a story. It advanced an investigation. That's a different thing entirely.

**Rating: 10/10 — The benchmark for the genre**

## Hunting Warhead: Journalism With Real Stakes

**Hunting Warhead** is a joint production between CBC (Canada) and Vice News about the takedown of the largest child sexual abuse material network ever discovered on the dark web.

Reporters Daemon Fairless and journalists embedded with Europol spent months inside the investigation, documenting how a small team of international law enforcement officers identified, tracked, and dismantled a network with hundreds of thousands of members.

The material is genuinely harrowing — this is not casual listening. But the journalism is exceptional. It's reported from inside a live investigation, which creates a tension and immediacy that most true crime simply cannot achieve. And it matters: the victims of CSAM networks are real people whose exploitation is ongoing until someone stops it.

Hunting Warhead won the Canadian Podcast Award for Best Investigative Podcast. It deserved it.

**Rating: 9/10 — Not for everyone, but essential for those who can engage with the subject**

## The Missing Cryptoqueen: $4 Billion and Counting

The BBC's **The Missing Cryptoqueen** follows reporter Jamie Bartlett's investigation into Ruja Ignatova, the founder of OneCoin — a cryptocurrency that was, the evidence strongly suggests, a complete fraud from the beginning.

OneCoin raised approximately $4 billion from investors in 175 countries between 2014 and 2017. Ignatova vanished in October 2017, days before she was to meet US law enforcement. She has not been found.

Bartlett's investigation — including interviews with former OneCoin employees, victims across the world, and law enforcement — is genuinely original reporting. The podcast has contributed to ongoing international investigations and kept pressure on a case that might otherwise have been quietly abandoned.

**Rating: 9/10 — Among the best financial crime podcasts ever made**

## Missing and Murdered: Finding Cleo

CBC journalist Connie Walker's investigation into the disappearance of Cleo Semaganis Nicotine — a Cree girl taken from her family during Canada's Sixties Scoop and later reported missing — is among the finest works of journalism the podcast medium has produced.

The Sixties Scoop was a Canadian government policy from the 1960s through 1980s under which thousands of Indigenous children were removed from their families and placed with white foster or adoptive families. Cleo was one of them. When she disappeared as an adult, she was treated as just another missing person — not as a victim of an ongoing system of state harm.

Walker's investigation into Cleo's disappearance became an investigation into the Sixties Scoop itself. The podcast contributed to changes in how Canada officially recognises and investigates missing Indigenous women and girls.

**Rating: 9/10 — Journalism that changed policy**

## The Coldest Case in Laramie

The New York Times and Serial Productions collaborated on this investigation into the 1985 murder of Shelli Wiley at the University of Wyoming — a case that was officially closed with a conviction, but which journalist Kim Barker believed hadn't been fully investigated.

The resulting podcast is among the finest examples of institutional investigative journalism applied to the audio medium. The NYT and Serial bring resources — legal access, forensic consultants, sustained time on a single story — that independent podcasters can't match. The result is a rigorously sourced, patient examination of a conviction that raises serious questions.

**Rating: 8/10 — Essential for fans of the Serial model of investigative journalism**

## Killing Richard Glossip

Richard Glossip has been on Oklahoma's death row since 1998 for ordering the murder of his employer, Barry Van Treese. He has always maintained his innocence. Three execution dates have been stayed.

**Killing Richard Glossip** examines the evidence — the original trial, the testimony of the man who actually committed the murder, and the multiple pieces of evidence that suggest Glossip may have been wrongly convicted — with a rigour that most true crime doesn't attempt.

This is the kind of podcast that matters precisely because the stakes are highest: a man may be executed for a crime he didn't commit.

**Rating: 8/10 — Required listening for anyone interested in the death penalty and wrongful conviction**

## Undisclosed: Legal Analysis That Moves Cases

**Undisclosed** — hosted by attorney Rabia Chaudry (Adnan Syed's family friend and the person who brought his case to Sarah Koenig) and a team of lawyers and investigators — is the most legally rigorous podcast in the true crime space.

It applies serious legal analysis to cases the team believes are wrongful convictions: examining suppressed evidence, identifying Brady violations, and building public pressure on cases where justice has been denied. It's demanding — this is not casual listening — but for listeners who want to understand how the criminal justice system actually functions rather than how TV dramatises it, it's essential.

**Rating: 7/10 — Essential for legally-minded true crime listeners**

## What Distinguishes Genuine Investigation

The defining characteristics of investigative true crime podcasting:

**Original interviews.** The best investigative podcasts talk to people who haven't spoken publicly before — family members, former law enforcement, witnesses, survivors. If every person you hear in a podcast has already been quoted in a newspaper, you're listening to a retelling, not an investigation.

**Document work.** Court records, police files, FOIA releases, autopsy reports. Investigative podcasts cite their sources and do the document work that most crime reporters never bother with.

**Honest uncertainty.** Genuine investigation often produces uncertainty rather than resolution. The best investigative podcasters are honest when the evidence is ambiguous — a rarer quality than it should be.

**Consequence.** The best investigative podcasts change something. A case is reopened. A conviction is overturned. A policy is reformed. A missing person's family gets an answer. Not every investigation produces these outcomes, but the best ones are pursuing something concrete.
