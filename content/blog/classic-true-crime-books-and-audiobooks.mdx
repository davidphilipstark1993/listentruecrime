Every genre has a canon, and true crime's is surprisingly short. A handful of books, mostly written between the 1960s and the early 2000s, set the template that podcasts still follow: the immersive reconstruction, the investigator as protagonist, the victim's life set against the crime. If you have been listening for a while and wondered where it all started, this is the shelf.

This guide covers four classics, plus two further titles worth knowing, and tries to be useful about each: what it is, how it has aged, where it has problems, and who it suits. Several concern murders, including the deaths of a family and of several young women. We describe them factually and without graphic detail.

## The one that started the modern genre

### In Cold Blood — Truman Capote

Capote's book covers the 1959 murders of the Clutter family in Holcomb, Kansas, and the two men convicted of them. Published in 1966, it is widely credited with establishing the modern true crime book, and it remains one of the best-known examples of narrative non-fiction.

It also needs a caveat. Capote called it a non-fiction novel, and critics have long argued that he invented or reshaped scenes and dialogue. That does not diminish the writing, which is superb, but it means you should read it as a work of art built on a true story, not as a precise record. It is the book that later true crime writing most often responds to, which is reason enough to know it.

**Best for:** readers who want the origin of the genre and a masterclass in style.

## The courtroom account

### Helter Skelter — Vincent Bugliosi and Curt Gentry

Bugliosi prosecuted the Manson Family trial, and this is his account of the 1969 Tate–LaBianca murders, the investigation and the trial. It is one of the best-selling true crime books ever published, and it shaped how a generation understood the case.

It is a prosecutor's book, which means it is a prosecutor's version of events, including his theory of Manson's motive, which later writers have disputed. It is also extremely long. Read it as an informed insider's account and a historical document, and pair it with something more recent for balance. The [Manson podcast](/podcasts/manson) is a limited series that investigates Charles Manson, the Manson Family and the Tate–LaBianca murders, which we list as good if US true crime interests you. For the broader subject of cults, see our guide to the [best cult podcasts](/blog/best-cult-podcasts).

**Best for:** readers who want the definitive prosecution narrative of a landmark case and are comfortable with its length.

## The one with two stories

### The Devil in the White City — Erik Larson

Larson tells two histories side by side: the construction of the 1893 Chicago World's Fair, and the killer H. H. Holmes, who operated in the city at the same time. It reads like a novel and it was a bestseller for good reason.

One point is worth knowing. The number of victims attributed to Holmes has been disputed by historians, and some popular accounts of his crimes are exaggerated. The book is best taken as narrative history, with the architecture of the fair as the strongest strand. It is a good choice for listeners who like a mix of true crime and history.

**Best for:** readers who prefer history to case files.

## The modern classic

### Killers of the Flower Moon — David Grann

Grann's book covers the murders of members of the Osage Nation in 1920s Oklahoma, and the early FBI investigation that followed. Published in 2017, it is the most recent book on this list and the easiest to start with. It moves in order, its structure is clear, and the human cost stays in view.

That structure suits listening well, and there is an audiobook edition we have confirmed on Amazon UK. We do not have a podcast on the site that covers the same ground, and we would rather say so than force a pairing.

**Best for:** a first book in the genre, or a first audiobook.

## Two more worth knowing about

We have not linked these, but they belong in the conversation.

***Wiseguy* by Nicholas Pileggi** is the life of the mob associate Henry Hill, and the basis for the film *Goodfellas*. It is told largely from Hill's own account, with all the self-serving qualities that implies. For related listening, see [Gangland Wire](/podcasts/gangland-wire), in which a former FBI agent covers organised crime from his years in the Bureau.

***Just Mercy* by Bryan Stevenson** is a civil rights lawyer's account of representing death-row prisoners and people wrongly convicted, centred on the case of Walter McMillian. It is closer to advocacy than to a conventional true crime book, and it is better for it. Our guide to [wrongful conviction podcasts](/blog/wrongful-conviction-podcasts) is the natural companion.

## Where the canon falls short

The classics are worth knowing, and they also have blind spots. Most were written in the mid-twentieth century or later by American men, and most centre the offender, the prosecutor or the writer himself. The people who were harmed often appear as figures in someone else's story, and the voices of people most affected by the justice system are largely absent.

Newer books have worked to correct this. *The Five*, which we cover in our guide to [British true crime books](/blog/best-british-true-crime-books-and-audiobooks), is about the women who were killed rather than the killer. *Just Mercy* is about the people the justice system failed. *Killers of the Flower Moon* is about a community under attack. None of this makes the older books less worth reading, but it is a useful reminder to ask whose story you are being told, and whose is missing.

## How these books fit with podcasts

The canon matters because podcasts inherited its habits. Serialised shows such as [Serial](/podcasts/serial) are the modern descendants of *In Cold Blood*'s immersive reconstruction, and our piece on [how Serial changed podcasting](/blog/how-serial-changed-podcasting) traces that line. The investigative strand runs from Grann to shows like [In the Dark](/podcasts/in-the-dark), which we rate a **Must listen** with a binge factor of 10.

Reading the older books also teaches you to question the newer ones. Capote's invented scenes and Bugliosi's confident theory are good examples of what a narrator can get away with. If you want to think about that more, the [ethics of true crime podcasting](/blog/ethics-of-true-crime-podcasting) is worth a read.

## What to expect from older books

Be ready for a different pace. These books were written for readers with no podcasts, no documentaries and long evenings, and they take their time. Long descriptive passages, extended courtroom sections and slow openings are normal. If a book feels slow in the first few chapters, give it a little longer, because the payoff in the classics is usually in the accumulation of detail. And if it still does not suit you, the audiobook editions are a good alternative, since a good narrator can carry a long book that is hard to read.

## A suggested order

If you are not sure where to begin, this is a reasonable path.

1. **Killers of the Flower Moon**, because it is the easiest and most rewarding.
2. **In Cold Blood**, because it is short enough to finish and important enough to know.
3. **The Devil in the White City**, if you want history alongside the crime.
4. **Helter Skelter**, when you are ready for something long.
