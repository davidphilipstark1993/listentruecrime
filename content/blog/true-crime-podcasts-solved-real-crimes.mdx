Most true crime podcasts document crimes. A small number have done something more: they've generated the evidence, attention, or legal pressure that directly contributed to an arrest, a conviction, a reversal, or a legal ruling.

This is a short list. True crime is far more often journalism about failures than journalism that corrects them. But these cases demonstrate what audio journalism can do when reporting is rigorous, sustained, and willing to follow evidence to uncomfortable conclusions.

---

## Teacher's Pet: The Conviction of Chris Dawson

**Case:** The 1982 disappearance of Lynette Dawson, Sydney
**Outcome:** Conviction of Chris Dawson for murder, 2022

In 1982, Lynette Dawson disappeared from her home in Bayview, Sydney. Her husband — former rugby league player and PE teacher Chris Dawson — became the prime suspect almost immediately, but was never charged. Police investigations in 1982 and 2003 went nowhere. Chris Dawson continued living in Australia, remarrying and maintaining his innocence.

In 2018, Australian journalist Hedley Thomas began investigating the case for The Australian newspaper and released the findings as a podcast. Teacher's Pet documented everything: the witnesses who said they'd seen Lynette after she disappeared (and were later shown to have lied or been mistaken), the teenage student Dawson had been having an affair with, the failures of NSW Police to properly investigate, and the accounts of women who said Dawson had abused them.

The podcast generated 700,000 downloads on its first day. It prompted an inquest and then a formal homicide investigation. NSW Police charged Chris Dawson in December 2018 — 36 years after Lynette disappeared. He was convicted of murder in August 2022 and sentenced to 24 years in prison.

Teacher's Pet is the clearest example in the genre of a podcast directly producing a criminal conviction.

## Your Own Backyard: The Conviction of Paul Flores

**Case:** The 1996 disappearance of Kristin Smart, California
**Outcome:** Conviction of Paul Flores for murder, 2022

Kristin Smart was 19 when she vanished after a campus party at Cal Poly San Luis Obispo in May 1996. The prime suspect from the beginning — student Paul Flores, who was the last person seen walking with Smart — was investigated but never charged. The case went cold.

Music teacher and amateur investigator Chris Lambert became obsessed with the case and in 2018 began releasing Your Own Backyard, documenting his own investigation alongside the historical record. Lambert interviewed witnesses who had been reluctant to talk to police, identified inconsistencies in accounts, and generated sustained public attention on the case.

The podcast was directly credited with bringing in new tips and witnesses. Law enforcement monitored the investigation. In April 2021, Paul Flores was arrested for Smart's murder. In October 2022, he was convicted and sentenced to 25 years to life. Paul's father, Ruben Flores, was acquitted of the accessory charge — but the primary conviction was obtained, 26 years after Kristin Smart disappeared.

Chris Lambert, a music teacher with no journalism training, became one of the few independent podcasters whose investigation contributed directly to a conviction.

## In the Dark: Supreme Court Ruling in Flowers v. Mississippi

**Case:** The trial of Curtis Flowers, Winona, Mississippi
**Outcome:** Supreme Court ruling vacating Flowers' sixth conviction, 2019

Curtis Flowers was tried six times for a 1996 quadruple murder in Winona, Mississippi — all by the same prosecutor, District Attorney Doug Evans. Three trials ended in hung juries. Three convictions were overturned by the Mississippi Supreme Court for prosecutorial misconduct.

Madeleine Baran and the APM Reports team spent two years investigating the case. Their statistical analysis documented that Evans struck Black jurors from the jury pool at 4.4 times the rate of white jurors over 25 years of prosecutorial work. They submitted this analysis in an amicus brief to the Supreme Court.

In 2019, the Supreme Court ruled 7-2 in Flowers v. Mississippi that Evans had violated the Equal Protection Clause in the sixth trial's jury selection. Flowers' conviction was vacated. After spending 23 years in prison — six trials — Curtis Flowers was released in 2020.

In the Dark Season 2 is the gold standard for the intersection of audio journalism and legal impact.

## Bear Brook: Four Victims Identified

**Case:** Four unidentified murder victims in New Hampshire, 1985-2000
**Outcome:** Identification of victims through genetic genealogy, 2019

Between 1985 and 2000, the decomposed remains of four female victims were found in barrels in New Hampshire forests. For decades they were Jane Does — no one knew who they were or who had killed them.

New Hampshire Public Radio journalist Lauren Chooljian covered the case in the Bear Brook podcast, which focused on the application of genetic genealogy to identify the victims. The podcast helped publicise the case and the technology, generating media attention that accelerated the identification efforts.

By 2019, three of the four victims had been identified using genetic genealogy. The suspected killer — a man believed to use multiple aliases — was also tentatively identified, though he was already dead. The fourth victim remains unidentified.

Bear Brook documented a significant development in forensic identification technology while contributing to the human resolution of a case that had been cold for three decades.

## Serial and the Adnan Syed Exoneration

**Case:** The 1999 murder of Hae Min Lee, Baltimore
**Outcome:** Vacation of Adnan Syed's conviction, 2022

Serial's relationship to the Adnan Syed case is the most complex on this list, because the podcast's actual contribution to the legal outcome is hard to quantify.

What is clear: Serial generated massive public attention on the case, which inspired further investigation by Undisclosed (the podcast by the three attorneys) and by Syed's legal team. Serial surfaced questions about the cell tower evidence, the inconsistency of key witnesses, and the performance of Syed's original counsel.

In 2022, Maryland prosecutors themselves moved to vacate Syed's conviction, acknowledging that they had withheld evidence about alternative suspects — specifically, a man named Ronald Lee Moore, who had a history of similar crimes and was known to police at the time of Hae Min Lee's murder.

The vacation of the conviction was based on prosecutorial failure, not on anything Serial established. But Serial kept the public attention on the case that eventually forced prosecutors to disclose evidence they should have disclosed two decades earlier. Whether that counts as "helping solve" the case is genuinely debatable.

## What This List Doesn't Include

Several shows have claimed credit for legal outcomes in ways that are harder to verify. Various podcasts have alleged that their investigation prompted tip submissions that led to arrests — claims that are often difficult to assess because law enforcement doesn't typically specify what information proved critical.

The cases above are limited to situations where the podcast's contribution to the legal outcome is documented and acknowledged — either by law enforcement, by the courts, or by the legal teams involved.

Most true crime podcasts have no direct legal impact. That's not a failure: journalism doesn't have to produce immediate legal outcomes to have value. Documenting systemic failures, maintaining public attention on cold cases, and giving victims and families a platform are all legitimate purposes.

But the shows on this list demonstrate that audio journalism can do more than document. In exceptional cases, it can change things.
