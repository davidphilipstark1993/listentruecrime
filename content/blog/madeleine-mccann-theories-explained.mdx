Almost twenty years after Madeleine McCann vanished from a holiday apartment in Praia da Luz, no theory of what happened to her has been proven in a court of law. That's easy to lose sight of given the sheer volume of speculation the case has generated across newspapers, forums, books, and now true crime podcasts. What follows is a straightforward account of the main explanations investigators and commentators have put forward — what evidence exists for each, and what doesn't.

None of what follows is presented as established fact. Operation Grange has never publicly confirmed which, if any, of these scenarios it regards as most likely, beyond treating a criminal act by someone outside the family as its working assumption.

## Stranger Abduction: The Working Theory

The theory that has driven the British investigation for most of its history is that Madeleine was taken by a stranger — most likely someone already in or near Praia da Luz that night, rather than a pre-planned abduction with a specific target. That's consistent with the resort's layout, the unlocked patio doors typical of the apartment block, and the fact that nothing has ever pointed to Madeleine being a deliberately chosen target in advance.

Since 2020, this theory has centred on [Christian Brueckner](/blog/who-is-christian-brueckner-madeleine-mccann), a German convicted sex offender who lived an itinerant life in and around Praia da Luz between 1995 and 2007, funding a transient existence partly through burglary. German prosecutors named him their prime suspect that year; Portuguese authorities made him a formal arguido in 2022. He has never been charged in Madeleine's case and continues to deny involvement. One piece of evidence cited publicly against him is a secondhand account, given to police in 2017, of a remark he allegedly made about a child not screaming when taken — treated by investigators as significant, though it falls well short of physical proof.

Brueckner's history of offending in the exact location, in the exact period, is what elevated him from one name among many to the case's central figure. But a pattern of offending, however troubling, isn't the same as evidence tying a specific person to a specific abduction.

## Burglary Gone Wrong: Scotland Yard's Alternative

A separate, not mutually exclusive, theory has periodically dominated the British side of the investigation: that Madeleine's disappearance began as a burglary that escalated when she woke. Scotland Yard pointed to a series of break-ins at the Ocean Club and nearby resorts in the months before May 2007, and treated a group of men active in the area as suspects for several years. In 2014, Portuguese police questioned several local men in connection with this theory; all were eventually ruled out, partly through mobile phone activity and their confirmed movements on the night in question.

This theory has never sat comfortably with Portuguese investigators. Carlos Anjos, a Portuguese police union official closely associated with the original case, dismissed it publicly, pointing out that nothing else was taken from the apartment — no wallet, no television, nothing beyond the child herself. That objection captures the theory's central weakness: it explains a plausible point of entry, but not why an opportunist thief would leave with a sleeping child rather than simply leaving.

## The Trafficking Theory

Around the ten-year anniversary of the case in 2017, British tabloids reported that Scotland Yard was pursuing leads connecting Madeleine's disappearance to a trafficking network. Then-Assistant Commissioner Mark Rowley confirmed detectives were pursuing a line of inquiry worth pursuing, without confirming its exact nature, and separately called the burglary-gone-wrong hypothesis "sensible" and under active consideration at the time.

No public evidence has ever substantiated the trafficking theory to the point of a named suspect or a confirmed route out of Portugal, and Operation Grange has never formally endorsed it as a leading hypothesis. It remains one of several explanations that would account for the total absence of a body, without itself being backed by anything more concrete than unattributed sourcing.

## The Theory That Implicated the Parents

The most controversial theory is the one most directly tied to Kate and Gerry McCann: that Madeleine died accidentally in the apartment and her parents concealed her body and staged an abduction. This is the theory advanced by Gonçalo Amaral, the Portuguese police inspector who led the original investigation until his removal in October 2007, in his book *A Verdade da Mentira*.

<Callout type="warning">
Portugal's attorney general formally cleared Kate and Gerry McCann of suspect status in July 2008 and archived the case for lack of evidence. When the McCanns later sued Amaral for libel, the courts — including, eventually, the European Court of Human Rights — ruled only that Amaral had a right under freedom-of-expression law to publish his opinion. No court ruled on whether the underlying allegation was true. Being legally publishable and being evidentially substantiated are two different things, and it's a distinction that gets lost often in online discussion of this case. ## Why Proving Any Theory Has Been So Difficult

Part of what keeps this case open to competing theories, rather than settled by one, is a set of practical obstacles that have nothing to do with which explanation is correct. Madeleine's body has never been found, which removes the single piece of evidence most likely to confirm or rule out any given scenario. The [original crime scene](/blog/portuguese-police-investigation-madeleine-mccann) was examined under Portuguese secrecy laws that limited early forensic transparency, and the investigation has had to work across three legal jurisdictions — Portugal, the UK, and, since 2020, Germany — with all the evidence-sharing and extradition complications that involves.

## Other Explanations

Beyond these central theories, the case has attracted a long tail of more speculative claims over the years — some based on misread evidence, others built almost entirely on online conjecture. These range from claims about specific sightings years after the disappearance to broader, unfounded narratives involving public figures, none of which have been supported by any police force involved in the case. Responsible coverage of this case treats that category with real caution: unlike the theories above, these accounts tend to cause direct, documented harm to the people they target without adding anything to the factual record.

## Where Investigators Stand

The abduction theory, with Christian Brueckner as its central figure, is the one receiving the most active investigative attention as of 2026. British detectives received a substantial dossier of German case files on Brueckner in 2026, and German prosecutors are separately seeking to overturn his October 2024 acquittal on unrelated sexual offence charges and secure a retrial. None of this amounts to proof. The honest answer to "what happened to Madeleine McCann" remains that nobody outside the person or people responsible knows for certain — which is exactly why the case continues to generate theories, and why those theories deserve to be weighed against the evidence rather than repeated as settled fact.
