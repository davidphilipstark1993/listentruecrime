Cults — or, more precisely, high-control groups — generate some of the most psychologically complex stories in true crime. The crime is often diffuse: systematic manipulation, controlled environments, exploitation, sometimes death. The leaders are often extraordinarily charismatic. And the question that drives every good cult podcast is the same one: *how did this happen to ordinary people?*

Every show below is in our catalogue, with a binge score and a full review on its own page. They are grouped by approach rather than strictly ranked, and we have kept to what each one actually covers.

---

## Cults

The broadest introduction to the subject, and the one we would start with. Hosted by Greg Polcyn and Vanessa Richardson, *Cults* explores the dark psychology of cults and cult leaders, covering cases from Jim Jones to modern groups. Because it moves from group to group, it works as a map of the genre: you will quickly learn which stories you want to hear in more depth.

It is episodic, with episodes of 35–55 minutes, so you can dip in without committing to a long arc.

## The Clearing

Amanda Knox and Jon Ronson investigate how cults recruit, isolate and destroy lives. Knox's own experience of wrongful conviction gives the series a dimension most cult podcasts lack: she understands, at a personal level, what it is to be held inside a story told about you by people with power.

## Heaven's Gate

Glynn Washington's nine-part investigation into the Heaven's Gate group and the 1997 mass suicide in which 39 members died, believing they would board an alien spacecraft. It is reported from inside the surviving community, which is what lifts it above a recitation of the facts: the people who knew the group describe a belief that felt real to them.

It is the right choice if you want a single group examined start to finish.

## Uncover (CBC)

CBC's investigative series pursues a different story of hidden wrongdoing each season. Season 1 is a foundational investigation into NXIVM, the self-help organisation that functioned as a sex cult under Keith Raniere, and it predates the Netflix documentary by a year. Later seasons turn to other cults and institutional abuses. Each season is six to eight episodes, so it is a manageable binge.

## Two-Faced: John of God

To thousands of people worldwide, João Teixeira de Faria was known as John of God, a Brazilian spiritual healer famous for performing procedures without anaesthetic. *Two-Faced*, from Exactly Right and iHeartPodcasts, unpacks the decades of alleged abuse hidden behind that reputation. It is a useful study in how a public reputation for healing can shield a group from scrutiny. Serialised, with episodes of 35–45 minutes.

## Understanding the psychology

What the best cult podcasts share is a commitment to answering the same question: how does this happen to people?

The answer, consistently, is that it does not happen the way the media tends to portray it. People do not join high-control groups because they are naive or weak. They join because those groups are designed to meet real human needs — community, meaning, certainty, identity — and the control arrives gradually, after the belonging.

That is also why survivors' voices matter so much in this genre. The most useful thing any of these shows does is make the process legible, so the next time it is easier to recognise.
