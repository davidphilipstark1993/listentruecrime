**Elizabeth Smart was 14 when she was taken from her bedroom in Salt Lake City, Utah, at knifepoint in the early hours of 5 June 2002.** Her abductors, a self-styled prophet named Brian David Mitchell and his wife, Wanda Barzee, held her for nine months before members of the public recognised Mitchell in March 2003 and called police. She was brought home alive. Today she is a mother of three, a campaigner for survivors of abuse, and the host of her own podcast. Netflix's documentary film *Kidnapped: Elizabeth Smart* was released on 21 January 2026.

If you've just watched the documentary, you've heard the story the way Elizabeth tells it, and that's the right place to start. This guide adds the context around it: the investigation and its missed chances, the long road to a trial, what happened to Mitchell and Barzee, and what Elizabeth has done since. Then we pick the podcasts worth your time next, with her own interviews kept separate from third-party coverage.

Facts here come from Netflix's official material, the FBI's Salt Lake City field office, contemporary reporting by NBC News, KSL and the Deseret News, and Elizabeth's own public interviews, checked on 25 November 2026. We use her own language about her experience where she has made it public, and we don't describe the abuse she suffered beyond what she has chosen to say.

## Who is Elizabeth Smart?

Elizabeth Ann Smart was born on 3 November 1987 in Salt Lake City, the second of six children of Ed and Lois Smart. She grew up in Federal Heights, a quiet neighbourhood near the University of Utah, in a close Latter-day Saint family. She played the harp.

That is the important starting point, and it's the one the Netflix film keeps returning to: before she was a news story, she was an ordinary teenager in an ordinary family. In interviews she has described growing up without imagining her life would be in any way unusual.

## What happened on 5 June 2002?

In the early hours of 5 June 2002, a man entered the Smart family home through a window and took Elizabeth from the bedroom she shared with her sister, Mary Katherine, threatening her with a knife.

Mary Katherine, who was nine, was awake. Terrified, she pretended to be asleep, and stayed hidden in bed for what may have been more than two hours before going to her parents shortly before 4am. A cut window screen confirmed what she was telling them. Elizabeth was gone.

Elizabeth was made to walk, in her pyjamas, several miles up into the foothills above the city to a camp where Wanda Barzee was waiting.

## Who are Brian David Mitchell and Wanda Barzee?

**Brian David Mitchell**, born in 1953, had a troubled history before he became a street preacher in Salt Lake City, where he called himself "Immanuel David Isaiah" and claimed to be a prophet. He had been excommunicated by the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. In late 2001 the Smart family had paid him for a few hours of roofing and yard work at their home, which is how he knew where they lived.

**Wanda Barzee**, born in 1945, married Mitchell in the 1980s and joined him in his religious delusions, taking the name "Hephzibah". She took an active part in holding Elizabeth captive.

Elizabeth has been clear that both of them were responsible for what happened to her, and that their religious language was a tool of control, not faith.

## The nine months of captivity

Mitchell and Barzee held Elizabeth first at a hidden camp in the foothills a few miles from her family's home, where she was at times tethered by a cable. Mitchell declared her his "wife". Elizabeth has said publicly, and testified in court, that he raped her repeatedly, and that she was threatened with the murder of her family if she tried to escape.

Over the following months they moved her in disguise, in robes and a veil, and she was seen in public several times, including at a Salt Lake City library in August 2002, where a detective who had been called approached the group. Mitchell said she was his daughter and refused to let the officer see her face. That autumn they took her to California, where they camped near Lakeside, east of San Diego.

Elizabeth has spoken repeatedly about why she did not run on those occasions, and why she rejects the idea of "Stockholm syndrome": she was a child, under constant threat, who had been told her family would be killed. Understanding that, rather than asking why she didn't escape, is central to how she tells her story now.

## The investigation and the national search

The search for Elizabeth was one of the biggest in Utah's history, with thousands of volunteers and saturation coverage on American national television.

Early on, police focused on **Richard Ricci**, a former handyman at the Smart home with a criminal record, who was jailed on an unrelated parole violation. He denied any involvement and was never charged in connection with the abduction. He died in custody of a brain haemorrhage in August 2002. Investigators also examined members of Elizabeth's own family, and found nothing.

The breakthrough came from the youngest witness. In October 2002, Mary Katherine suddenly realised whose voice she had heard that night: "Emmanuel", the street preacher who had worked on their house. Police were cautious, and according to Netflix, they held back from releasing a sketch for fear it might scare him away. In February 2003 the family released a sketch themselves, and it was shown on *Larry King Live* and *America's Most Wanted*. Mitchell's relatives recognised him and gave police photographs.

## How Elizabeth was found

By early 2003, Elizabeth had persuaded her captors, by appealing to Mitchell's own claims of divine instruction, to return to Utah. It's a detail she now speaks about with some pride: the moment she outmanoeuvred him.

On 12 March 2003, in Sandy, a suburb about 18 miles south of her family's home, people who had seen Mitchell's face in the coverage recognised him walking with a woman and a girl, and called police. Sandy officers stopped the group. Elizabeth, frightened and still under Mitchell's control, at first gave a false name. She was 15; her birthday had passed in captivity. Within hours she was reunited with her family.

## The arrests, the evidence and the convictions

Mitchell and Barzee were arrested on the day Elizabeth was found and charged in Utah's state courts. What followed took the best part of eight years. In the state case, Mitchell was repeatedly found incompetent to stand trial, and the courts ruled that the state could not force him to take medication. In 2008 federal prosecutors charged him instead.

The federal case rested above all on Elizabeth's own testimony, given in November 2010 when she was 23, alongside evidence from investigators and witnesses who had seen the group during her captivity. Mitchell's defence argued he was insane; the jury rejected it. On 10 December 2010 he was convicted of kidnapping and of transporting a minor across state lines for illegal sexual activity. On 25 May 2011, Judge Dale Kimball sentenced him to life in federal prison. Elizabeth spoke at the hearing and told him she would go on to live a good life.

Barzee pleaded guilty to federal charges in November 2009 and, in May 2010, was sentenced to 15 years. She was released in September 2018 after the Utah Board of Pardons recalculated her time served, a decision Elizabeth publicly opposed. She was arrested again in May 2025 for allegedly visiting parks in breach of Utah's sex offender restrictions; police said she told them she had been "commanded" to go there.

## Elizabeth's recovery and advocacy

Elizabeth has often said that the most important advice she received came from her mother soon after she came home: that what happened had taken nine months from her, and that she shouldn't give her captors a single second more. She went back to school, studied music at Brigham Young University, served a mission in Paris, and in 2011 founded the **Elizabeth Smart Foundation**, which works to prevent abuse and support survivors.

Since then she has:

- **Written two books.** *My Story* (2013, with Chris Stewart) is her full account of the abduction; *Where There's Hope* (2018) is about healing, drawing on conversations with other survivors.
- **Campaigned for change.** She has testified on child abuse prevention, spoken widely on how talk of "purity" can make survivors feel worthless, and set up Smart Defense, a self-defence programme, after being groped on a flight in 2019.
- **Produced her own story on screen.** She narrated and produced Lifetime's *I Am Elizabeth Smart* (2017) before taking part in Netflix's documentary.
- **Launched her own podcast.** *The Survivor Files*, from iHeartPodcasts, began in August 2026, with other survivors telling their stories at their own pace.

### Where is Elizabeth Smart now?

Elizabeth lives in Utah with her husband, Matthew Gilmour, whom she married in 2012, and their three children. She continues to run her foundation, speak publicly and host *The Survivor Files*. In recent interviews she has also talked about taking up competitive bodybuilding, and about how she talks to her own children about safety. In late 2026 she remains one of the most recognised advocates for survivors of abduction and sexual violence in the US.

## Netflix's Kidnapped: Elizabeth Smart: what the documentary covers

*Kidnapped: Elizabeth Smart* is a feature-length Netflix documentary directed by Benedict Sanderson and produced by the British company Minnow Films. It runs for 1 hour 31 minutes and was released on 21 January 2026. It is available on Netflix in the UK, where it is rated 15.

The film is told primarily by Elizabeth herself. According to Netflix, it also includes her sister **Mary Katherine**, her father **Ed Smart**, her uncles **Dave and Tom Smart**, detectives, reporters, and people in Utah who encountered Elizabeth while she was disguised during her captivity. Her mother, **Lois**, chose not to be interviewed; Elizabeth has said some family members want to leave that time in the past, and that she respects it.

### What's new or previously unseen

Netflix describes the film as using never-before-seen archival material alongside the new interviews. The most significant new element is the access: Elizabeth telling the story at length, in her own words, at 38, and Mary Katherine giving her full account of the night of the abduction and the months that followed. Elizabeth told Netflix she took part partly to "own" her story and to show people that "there are happy endings".

The film was a hit: Collider, citing third-party chart tracker FlixPatrol, reported that it reached number two on Netflix's global film chart in its first week.

## Is Kidnapped: Elizabeth Smart worth watching?

**Yes. It is one of the better true crime documentaries of 2026, largely because it lets the survivor lead.**

<Callout type="verdict">
*Kidnapped: Elizabeth Smart* is a conventionally built documentary that earns its place through Elizabeth's own testimony, in which she is clear, composed and in control of her own story. Critics have been consistently positive: it holds a 100% critics' score on Rotten Tomatoes from 12 reviews, with the Guardian's Lucy Mangan praising how firmly Elizabeth puts the responsibility where it belongs. If you've followed the case for years, you won't learn many new facts, but you will hear them from the people who lived them.
</Callout>

**Its strengths:**

- **The survivor leads.** Unlike the cable news coverage of 2002 and 2003, the film puts Elizabeth's account at the centre and lets her frame what happened, including why she didn't run.
- **Mary Katherine's story.** The film gives real space to the nine-year-old witness whose memory led to the rescue, and to what carrying that did to her.
- **The near-misses.** Interviews with people in Utah who saw Elizabeth in disguise, and the account of the library encounter, show how close she came to being found earlier, from the people who were there.

**Its limitations:**

- **It's conventional.** Reviewers including Melbourne's *The Age* described its structure as conventional. It is a classic interview-and-archive documentary rather than a formally adventurous one.
- **A 91-minute film has to choose.** Its focus is the abduction, the captivity and the rescue. For the eight-year legal fight that followed, including the competency battles, the section above and the podcasts below go further.
- **Not everyone is here.** Lois Smart's absence is noticeable but understandable, and the film is built on testimony rather than a reinvestigation of how the police handled the case.

## The bottom line

Elizabeth Smart was taken from her home at 14 and held for nine months by Brian David Mitchell and Wanda Barzee. She came home because her sister remembered a voice, her family refused to stop, and people who recognised her abductor picked up the phone. Mitchell is serving life; Barzee has served her sentence. Elizabeth has spent the two decades since turning her experience into support for other survivors. *Kidnapped: Elizabeth Smart* is worth watching because it lets her tell that story herself, and the best podcasts to follow it are the ones that do the same.
