Financial crime devastates more lives than violent crime. A Ponzi scheme can wipe out thousands of retirement accounts. A cryptocurrency fraud can transfer billions from ordinary investors to criminals. A corporate fraud can destroy entire companies and the communities built around them.

And yet the true crime genre — obsessively focused on murder — gives financial crime a fraction of the attention it deserves.

These are the podcasts that take fraud, scams, and white-collar crime as seriously as they merit.

## The Missing Cryptoqueen: The $4 Billion Fraud You've Never Heard Of

The BBC's **The Missing Cryptoqueen** is the most important fraud podcast ever made, and it covers the most important fraud you've probably never heard of.

OneCoin was founded by Bulgarian-born Ruja Ignatova in 2014 as a "Bitcoin killer" — a cryptocurrency that would outperform Bitcoin and make investors rich. It raised approximately $4 billion from investors in 175 countries before Ignatova vanished in October 2017.

The problem: OneCoin appears to have been a fraud from the beginning. There was no functioning blockchain. The "coins" had no real value. The returns promised to investors were generated entirely by new investor money.

Reporter Jamie Bartlett's investigation — which has contributed to multiple ongoing international investigations — is a masterpiece of financial crime journalism. And the central mystery (where is Ruja Ignatova now?) remains genuinely open.

**Rating: 9/10** — Essential listening

## Bad Blood: Silicon Valley's Greatest Fraud

Elizabeth Holmes founded Theranos in 2003 at age 19 with the promise of revolutionising medical testing. For a decade, she claimed that Theranos's devices could run hundreds of tests from a single drop of blood — faster, cheaper, and more accurately than traditional labs.

None of it was true.

**Bad Blood** (based on John Carreyrou's Wall Street Journal reporting and subsequent book) covers one of Silicon Valley's most spectacular frauds, involving falsified test results, a star-studded board of directors who failed to ask basic questions, and a cover-up that put patients' lives at risk.

Holmes was convicted of fraud in 2022 and sentenced to 11 years in federal prison. Her trial and conviction are documented in the Bad Blood Podcast and its companion materials.

**Rating: 8/10** — Among the most significant corporate crime podcasts ever produced

## Scam Goddess: Comedy Meets Financial Justice

**Scam Goddess** is the best comedy-true crime podcast that focuses on fraud rather than violence. Host Laci Mosley covers scams, con artists, and fraudsters with a comedic sensibility that never loses sight of the genuine harm done to victims.

The format works because Mosley is genuinely funny — not in the way that laughs at victims, but in the way that exposes the absurdity of the situations fraud creates and the failure of institutions to prevent it. She's also angry, which is appropriate: many of the scams she covers were preventable, and the systems that should have stopped them didn't.

**Rating: 7/10** — One of the best comedy-true crime podcasts in the genre

## Scamfluencers: Social Media, Fraud, and the New Con Artist

**Scamfluencers** (Wondery) examines the intersection between social media influence and fraud. The Fyre Festival. Fake wellness gurus. Instagram investment schemes. Online "coaches" selling imaginary expertise.

Social media has created a new class of fraud that didn't exist before: the influencer-driven scam, which exploits parasocial relationships and aspirational lifestyles to extract money from people who feel they know and trust the fraudster.

The podcast examines why these scams work — which is a more interesting question than the mechanics of how they operate.

**Rating: 6/10** — Smart and entertaining

## Pretend: The Psychology of Deception

**Pretend** takes a different approach: instead of focusing on the crime, it focuses on the psychology. Why do people deceive? What cognitive vulnerabilities do con artists exploit? What does the act of deception reveal about the deceiver?

The case studies are drawn from both contemporary and historical fraud, and the analysis is genuinely rigorous. This is true crime for listeners who are interested in human psychology as much as crime.

**Rating: 7/10** — Excellent for psychologically-minded listeners

## Smoke Screen: Fake Priest

A man posed as a Catholic priest for years across multiple American cities, collecting donations, performing ceremonies, and earning the trust of parishes before the fraud unravelled.

**Smoke Screen: Fake Priest** is the most peculiar fraud story in this genre: the con artist wasn't seeking enormous sums of money in a single score but rather the sustained status and community of a religious institution. It raises interesting questions about what fraud is really about when the primary reward isn't financial.

**Rating: 7/10** — Genuinely strange and gripping

## How Financial Fraud Works: What Good Podcasts Understand

The best financial crime podcasts understand several things that mediocre ones miss:

**Fraud exploits trust, not gullibility.** The victims of major frauds aren't naive. Bernie Madoff's victims included sophisticated institutional investors and very experienced individuals who had spent careers in finance. What fraud exploits is the human tendency to rely on social proof and institutional endorsement — which is rational in most situations and dangerous when the institution is fraudulent.

**Fraud is usually enabled by institutions.** Theranos raised $900 million from venture capitalists who didn't perform basic due diligence. OneCoin was promoted through multi-level marketing networks that created social pressure. The question "how were these people fooled?" usually has the answer "because the systems that should have checked didn't."

**Financial harm is real harm.** The framing of financial crime as less serious than violent crime ignores the devastation that fraud causes: retirement savings wiped out, businesses destroyed, communities damaged. For elderly victims of investment fraud, the loss of life savings is a catastrophic harm with no path to recovery.

**Regulatory failure is almost always present.** Major frauds almost always involve periods when regulators were given evidence and failed to act. Madoff was reported to the SEC multiple times before his exposure in 2008. Theranos's test results were reported as suspicious by laboratory professionals long before regulators intervened.

## The Financial Crimes Worth More Coverage

Several major financial crime stories don't yet have the definitive podcast treatment they deserve:

**The Panama Papers** — The 2016 leak of 11.5 million documents from Mossack Fonseca revealed how the world's wealthy use offshore structures to avoid tax. Podcast coverage has been fragmented.

**The 1MDB scandal** — $4.5 billion stolen from a Malaysian sovereign wealth fund, with connections to Goldman Sachs, Hollywood film production, and the Jho Low who allegedly orchestrated it all. Some excellent journalism exists; no truly definitive podcast.

**Wirecard** — German payment processing company that collapsed in 2020 after it was revealed that €1.9 billion in "cash" on its books didn't exist. One of the biggest accounting frauds in European history.

The best of the existing financial crime podcasts show that this material can be as gripping as any murder case. There's room for much more.
