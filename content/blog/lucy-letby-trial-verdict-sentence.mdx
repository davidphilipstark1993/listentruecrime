Lucy Letby's trial at Manchester Crown Court ran for close to ten months, making it one of the longest criminal trials in modern English legal history. The jury that eventually convicted her spent 22 days in deliberation before returning its first verdicts on 18 August 2023 — one of the longest jury deliberations on record in England and Wales, reflecting both the volume of medical evidence involved and the number of individual counts jurors were asked to weigh.

## Ten Months at Manchester Crown Court

Letby's trial opened in October 2022 before Mr Justice James Goss, with an original jury of eight women and four men. She faced 22 charges relating to 17 babies: seven counts of murder and fifteen of attempted murder. She denied every charge. Over the following months, jurors heard from dozens of witnesses — treating doctors and nurses describing individual collapses in granular clinical detail, forensic and medical experts assessing likely causes, grieving parents, and eventually Letby herself — across the equivalent of nine months of live evidence.

## The Prosecution's Case

Prosecutor Nick Johnson KC built the Crown's case around eleven "common factors" linking the seventeen incidents under consideration, rather than any single piece of direct evidence — among them Letby's presence on shift for every incident, unusual and unexplained skin discolouration reported in multiple babies, and a pattern of collapses shortly after parents had visited and left the ward. Johnson told jurors the collapses were not "naturally occurring tragedies" but the deliberate work of "the woman in the dock." Central to this argument was that Letby's presence during each incident couldn't credibly be explained by coincidence alone, given the statistical unlikelihood of one nurse being present for so many unexpected deteriorations across an eighteen-month period.

## The Defence Case

Defence barrister Ben Myers KC rejected the prosecution's common factors as failing to establish any actual pattern of deliberate harm, arguing the underlying clinical presentations were consistent with the known risks of caring for extremely premature and critically unwell infants. The defence pointed to documented problems with hygiene standards and staffing levels on the unit, including evidence it was at times operating without adequate senior medical cover overnight, and argued specific pieces of evidence — including some of the skin discolouration described by treating clinicians — hadn't been consistently or contemporaneously recorded.

Mr Justice Goss told the jury in his summing up that the prosecution's case rested "substantially, but not wholly" on circumstantial evidence — a description that has taken on added significance since 2025, as some of that circumstantial reasoning has come under sustained challenge from medical experts. ## Letby Gives Evidence

Letby gave evidence in her own defence over multiple days, denying every allegation. She was questioned in detail about the handwritten notes recovered from her home — covered in full in [our piece on how she was caught](/blog/how-lucy-letby-was-caught) — offering explanations that differed from the prosecution's "confession" framing, and was cross-examined at length on her presence at each incident, her online search history, and inconsistencies the prosecution identified between her clinical notes and other staff members' records. At points during cross-examination she became visibly distressed, and the trial was paused more than once to allow her time to compose herself.

## 22 Days of Deliberation

The jury retired after Mr Justice Goss directed them to approach their task "in a fair, calm, objective and analytical way," reminding them they needed to be sure of guilt on each individual count rather than reaching a single overall judgment about Letby's character. Deliberations ran to 22 days. Partway through, one juror was discharged for personal reasons, reducing the panel to eleven; the judge subsequently permitted the remaining jurors to reach verdicts on a majority basis — ten of eleven in agreement.

## 18 August 2023: The Verdicts

The jury returned its first guilty verdicts on 18 August 2023: seven counts of murder and seven of attempted murder. Letby was acquitted on two further counts of attempted murder, and the jury couldn't agree on the remaining six, including one relating to Child K that would later be retried.

The split verdict is worth dwelling on, because it's sometimes lost in later characterisations of the case as entirely clear-cut. The same jury heard essentially the same general evidence about Letby's conduct, character and opportunity across all 22 counts, yet reached three distinct outcomes depending on the specific facts of each individual allegation — a pattern suggesting a jury weighing each charge on its own evidential merits, rather than reasoning from a single overall impression of guilt.

## 21 August 2023: A Whole-Life Sentence

Letby was sentenced three days later. She refused to attend the hearing, prompting Mr Justice Goss to proceed by addressing her as though she were present. He described her offending as "a cruel, calculated and cynical campaign of child murder involving the smallest and most vulnerable of children," said there had been "a malevolence bordering on sadism," and found no mitigating factors and no remorse.

The whole-life order — the most severe sentence available under English law, carrying no possibility of parole — made Letby only the fourth woman in British legal history to receive one, following Myra Hindley, Rosemary West and Joanna Dennehy. Her case drew immediate comparison with that of Beverley Allitt, a nurse convicted in 1991 of murdering four children and attempting to murder three more at a Lincolnshire hospital.

Letby's refusal to attend her sentencing prompted significant political reaction. Then-Prime Minister Rishi Sunak called it "cowardly," and confirmed the government intended to legislate to compel attendance at sentencing hearings in future cases. Several parents read victim impact statements regardless, including the mother of Child I, who described her daughter as having been "tortured till she had no fight left in her."

## The Retrial: A Single Count, a Second Verdict

Letby's first application for permission to appeal her fourteen convictions was refused in 2024. Separately, prosecutors retried her on the single remaining count: the attempted murder of Child K in February 2016, allegedly by dislodging her breathing tube shortly after birth. The retrial, before the same judge, ran for around four weeks and leaned heavily on the account of a colleague nurse who said she'd seen Letby standing over Child K's incubator despite not being the baby's designated carer that night. The jury returned a guilty verdict on 2 July 2024, and Letby was sentenced on 5 July 2024 to a fifteenth whole-life order, run concurrently with the others. Her subsequent application to appeal this conviction too was refused in October 2024.

## How the Trial's Findings Are Being Revisited

The verdicts returned in 2023 and 2024 remain the legal record. What's changed since is the weight now attached to some of the medical reasoning that underpinned them, following the February 2025 intervention by Dr Shoo Lee's international panel and the ongoing Criminal Cases Review Commission review. Mr Justice Goss's own description of the case as resting "substantially, but not wholly" on circumstantial evidence remains, in that sense, the most precise summary available of both the strength and the vulnerability of the case as it was originally tried.
