**Rachel Nickell, a 23-year-old mother, was killed in front of her two-year-old son on Wimbledon Common on 15 July 1992. The man police pursued for her murder, Colin Stagg, was not the killer: the case against him collapsed at the Old Bailey in 1994, and he has since been compensated and publicly apologised to. The man who killed her, Robert Napper, was already detained in Broadmoor, and in December 2008 he pleaded guilty to her manslaughter on the grounds of diminished responsibility.** Netflix's documentary *The Murder of Rachel Nickell* was released on 4 June 2026.

If the film is how you found this case, this guide covers what happened, how the investigation went wrong, how it was eventually solved, what the Netflix documentary does and doesn't show, and the podcasts that tell the story best, with verified episode links. It is written with care for Rachel, her partner André and her son Alex, who lived this, and we have kept distressing detail to what is needed to understand the case.

### In short

- Rachel was killed in daylight on a well-used part of the Common, with her toddler beside her.
- Police fixed on Colin Stagg and, with no forensic evidence against him, ran an undercover "honeytrap". The judge threw it out in 1994.
- While Stagg was awaiting trial, Napper killed Samantha Bisset and her four-year-old daughter Jazmine. Detectives on that case saw the similarities. The Nickell team, who already had Stagg, did not act on them.
- A cold-case review using new DNA techniques identified Napper as Rachel's killer, and he admitted manslaughter in 2008.
- The IPCC later found that police had missed chances to stop Napper years before.

## Who was Rachel Nickell?

Rachel Nickell was 23, a former model and a mother ([Police Professional](https://policeprofessional.com/?p=97895)). She lived in south London with her partner, André Hanscombe, a tennis coach, and their son Alex, who was two. [The Independent reported](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/uk/crime/how-life-changed-grieving-relatives-and-blundering-police-1203684.html) that André met Rachel at a party and that they moved into a flat in Balham together within months.

Her parents, Andrew and Monica Nickell, spoke publicly only rarely. In a statement read at the Old Bailey in 2008 they said: "We hope the man who committed the crime will spend the rest of his life in prison. That is the sentence he has given us."

## What happened on Wimbledon Common?

On the morning of 15 July 1992 Rachel took Alex and the family dog for a walk on Wimbledon Common. She was attacked, sexually assaulted and killed in broad daylight. A dog walker came across Alex, who was still with his mother ([Netflix](https://www.netflix.com/tudum/articles/the-murder-of-rachel-nickell-release-date-news)). The Old Bailey heard in 2008 that he was holding her arm, crying and saying: "Get up, Mummy."

The murder of a young mother in an ordinary park on a summer morning shocked the country, and it put the Metropolitan Police under enormous pressure to find her killer quickly: [Crime + Investigation](https://crimeandinvestigation.co.uk/crime-files/rachel-nickell-case/trial) describes officers as under "incredible pressure from the press and their own bosses". That pressure runs through everything that followed.

## Alex and André Hanscombe

Alex was a month short of his third birthday ([The Independent](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/uk/crime/how-life-changed-grieving-relatives-and-blundering-police-1203684.html)). He was the only witness, and far too young to tell detectives what had happened. According to Netflix's account, police asked André's permission to question him with the help of a child psychologist. After repeated interviews and a visit to the scene that upset the little boy further, André decided Alex should take no further part in the investigation.

André and Alex later moved abroad, first to France and then to Spain, to escape press attention. André wrote about the experience in *The Last Thursday in July* (1996), and Alex's memoir *Letting Go* (2017) is the basis for Netflix's drama *The Witness*. Both consulted on the documentary and the drama.

Alex has been outspoken about the police response. He told Netflix: "They failed to take the killer off the street years before the attack on my mother and me... The police have never acknowledged the extent of their failings or the wrongs they did in a true spirit of contrition." Those are his words and his view, and the IPCC's findings, covered below, support part of what he says.

Rachel's parents, too, said in their statement that their lives had been "trampled on by the media". Press intrusion on the family is part of this story, and part of why we have kept this guide restrained.

## The initial police investigation

The Met launched what [Crime + Investigation](https://crimeandinvestigation.co.uk/crime-files/rachel-nickell-case/trial) calls the largest murder investigation in British history. [The Independent reported](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/uk/crime/how-life-changed-grieving-relatives-and-blundering-police-1203684.html) that officers identified more than 6,000 potential suspects and made 34 arrests. Detective Inspector Keith Pedder was in day-to-day charge.

There was almost nothing to go on forensically. According to [Crime + Investigation](https://crimeandinvestigation.co.uk/crime-files/rachel-nickell-case/trial), a minute speck of possible organic material was found, but 1992 science could not extract a DNA profile from it. Police brought in the forensic psychologist Paul Britton to produce a profile of the likely killer, and a profile and photofit were broadcast in a national TV appeal. At least four callers named Colin Stagg.

## Colin Stagg and the wrongful suspicion

### How a local man became the main suspect

Colin Stagg lived near the Common. In 1992 he had been fined £200 for indecent exposure there, an offence he has said arose from nude sunbathing in a secluded spot ([The Independent](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/uk/crime/how-life-changed-grieving-relatives-and-blundering-police-1203684.html); [Crime + Investigation](https://crimeandinvestigation.co.uk/crime-files/rachel-nickell-case/trial)). Police saw that, and his interest in the occult, as matching Britton's profile. There was no forensic evidence linking him to the murder, and he never confessed.

### The honeytrap: Operation Edzell

With no evidence, police launched an undercover operation, named Operation Edzell. A woman officer, using the pseudonym "Lizzie James", contacted Stagg through a lonely hearts column and wrote to him and spoke to him over a period of months, encouraging him to share sexual fantasies. [Police Professional](https://policeprofessional.com/?p=102097) describes it as "a classic honey trap" devised with Britton's help, in which a policewoman befriended a sexually inexperienced man with the promise of intimacy if he confessed.

The exchange that police themselves released tells its own story. In a recorded call, the officer said: "If only you had done the Wimbledon Common murder, if only you had killed her, it would be all right." Stagg replied: "I'm terribly sorry, but I haven't." He was arrested and charged in August 1993 and spent about 13 months in custody.

### Why the case collapsed

At the Old Bailey in September 1994, Mr Justice Ognall ruled the evidence from the operation inadmissible. Netflix and [NZ Herald reporting](https://nzherald.co.nz/world/cops-botched-the-case/SGO4HK4ZC3GQK5NO7AHKJJOWNA) quote him describing "deceptive conduct of the grossest kind", and The Independent reported him calling it a "blatant attempt to incriminate a suspect". No jury was ever sworn in. With no other evidence, the prosecution withdrew and Stagg walked free.

<Callout type="warning">
**Colin Stagg was never convicted of Rachel Nickell's murder.** He was charged, the evidence was thrown out, and the case ended. He was later paid compensation by the Home Office, and the Metropolitan Police publicly apologised to him. Robert Napper, not Stagg, killed Rachel. If you see any coverage that blurs this, it is wrong.
</Callout>

### After the acquittal

Walking free did not end it. Stagg told Netflix he remained "Britain's most hated person for about 15 years". In August 2008 the Home Office awarded him £706,000 under a discretionary compensation scheme. The independent assessor, Lord Brennan QC, concluded: "I am quite satisfied that this amounts to misconduct in the investigation and prosecution of this case and I categorise it egregious" ([Police Professional](https://policeprofessional.com/?p=97895)). After Napper's plea on 18 December 2008, Assistant Commissioner John Yates apologised to Stagg publicly. "I apologise to him for the mistakes that were made in the early 1990s," he said, "and we also recognise the huge and lasting impact that this has had on his life."

The officer who played "Lizzie James" later left the police and received £125,000 in damages from the Met, according to [The Independent](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/uk/crime/how-life-changed-grieving-relatives-and-blundering-police-1203684.html). Paul Britton has said he told police early on that Rachel's killer and the Bisset killer were the same man; police disagree, according to The Independent. [Police Professional](https://policeprofessional.com/?p=102097) reports him as saying he told police Stagg was not their man. A professional misconduct complaint against him to the British Psychological Society was dismissed in 2002 ([Crime + Investigation](https://crimeandinvestigation.co.uk/crime-files/rachel-nickell-case/trial)).

## The role of forensic evidence

**In 1992 there was no forensic evidence against Stagg, and the science wasn't ready.** The key biological material was too small to profile. The Met's case against him therefore rested on psychology and on what he wrote to an undercover officer, not on forensics.

**By the 2000s, it identified Napper.** After a cold-case review began in 2002, forensic scientists developed techniques to work with very small samples. [Netflix describes](https://www.netflix.com/tudum/articles/the-murder-of-rachel-nickell-release-date-news) the team spending two years developing a method to multiply DNA found at the scene.

**There were forensic failures too.** According to [The Telegraph](https://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/uknews/3813201/Rachel-Nickell-The-missed-opportunities-to-catch-killer-Robert-Napper.html), in 2001 the Forensic Science Service reviewed the evidence but missed traces of DNA that another laboratory, LGC, found three years later. LGC also found that Napper had left a footprint at the scene that had been ignored because it was assumed to be too small, when in fact it had shrunk because the soil was wet. The FSS's admission led to a review of up to 4,000 rape and homicide investigations.

**Profiling was tested and found wanting.** According to the *Listening to the Dead* podcast, the fallout from this case and another led to offender profiling being questioned and mistrusted by police and the public for some years. Its episode, listed [below](#recommended-listening-order), discusses it with a working behavioural investigative advisor.

For how DNA has reopened other cases, see our guide to [genetic genealogy cold cases](/blog/genetic-genealogy-cold-cases).

## The investigation's failures and missed opportunities

This is the heart of the story, and it is why the case is still discussed. The Telegraph counted [six key opportunities](https://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/uknews/3813201/Rachel-Nickell-The-missed-opportunities-to-catch-killer-Robert-Napper.html) to stop Napper:

1. **1989: his mother's report.** She told police her son had confessed to raping a woman on Plumstead Common. Police could not trace the crime and the lead was not followed up. The IPCC accepted that the call was made, though police have no record of it.
2. **1992: identified by neighbours.** After a photofit of the "Green Chain" rapist was issued, neighbours named Napper. He was asked to give a DNA sample and twice failed to attend, but was ruled out because detectives had decided to eliminate suspects over 6ft tall. The IPCC commissioner said police "inconceivably" eliminated him on that basis.
3. **October 1992: a weapons find.** After he asked a printer to copy Met Police notepaper, his home was searched and police found weapons. A psychiatric report called him an immediate threat to himself and the public. He served about eight weeks for firearms offences. A map book found at his address, which later turned out to mark attack sites, was not understood at the time.
4. **1993: seen near a house in Plumstead.** An officer wrote that he was "strange, abnormal" and "should be considered possible rapist". He was allowed to leave.
5. **1994: the Bisset team's warning.** After Napper's arrest, detectives on the Bisset case put his name forward to the Nickell team, who had already charged Stagg.
6. **2001: the missed DNA.** The forensic review that missed the traces described above.

### What the IPCC found

In June 2010, after a complaint by André Hanscombe, the IPCC published its findings. Commissioner Rachel Cerfontyne said: "It is clear that throughout the investigations into the 'Green Chain' rapes and Rachel Nickell's death there was a catalogue of bad decisions and errors made by the Metropolitan Police." She added: "Without these errors, Robert Napper could have been off the streets before he killed Rachel Nickell and the Bissets, and before numerous women suffered violent sexual attacks at his hands" ([BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/10216328)).

The IPCC also said it had received fresh information that Napper had come to a police sergeant's attention as a "serious threat to women" in the mid-1980s, and that a man said he had told police two months after Rachel's death that he had overheard Napper and a friend laughing about the killing. No officer faced discipline: they had all retired, and one key senior detective had died.

The Met said it accepted that "more could, and should have been done", and Assistant Commissioner Cressida Dick wrote to André and Alex to apologise again. André Hanscombe said that having had time to come to terms with the findings, "the best way to serve those who paid most heavily is to make sure all the lessons have been learned" ([BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/10216328)).

### Why the Bisset detectives weren't heard

The most painful part is the link nobody made in time. When Samantha and Jazmine Bisset were killed in November 1993, detectives on that case saw similarities with Rachel's murder. The Nickell team already had Stagg in custody awaiting trial and did not take the connection further. Samantha Bisset's partner, Conrad Ellam, told the BBC at the time of Napper's plea that police told him there were "too many differences", and that they were focused on Stagg. [Commander Simon Foy of the Met later said](https://nzherald.co.nz/world/cops-botched-the-case/SGO4HK4ZC3GQK5NO7AHKJJOWNA) that if any of the earlier opportunities had been taken, "it is probable that he would have been in custody and would not have murdered Samantha and Jazmine."

## Robert Napper and the eventual breakthrough

Robert Napper was born in 1966 and lived in Plumstead, south-east London. Police came to believe he was responsible for a long series of sexual attacks in the area in the late 1980s and early 1990s, known as the Green Chain rapes (reported figures vary, but run to more than a hundred incidents).

He was arrested in May 1994 over the Bisset killings and in October 1995 pleaded guilty to the manslaughter of Samantha and Jazmine on the grounds of diminished responsibility, and to related sexual offences. He was detained in Broadmoor. When Met detectives interviewed him there about Rachel's murder in December 1995, he declined to discuss it without forensic evidence ([NZ Herald](https://nzherald.co.nz/world/cops-botched-the-case/SGO4HK4ZC3GQK5NO7AHKJJOWNA)).

The breakthrough came through science, not detective work. A cold-case review began in 2002. As described above, forensic scientists found traces that earlier tests had missed. [PA reported](https://www.irishtimes.com/news/uk-police-issue-apology-as-rachel-nickell-s-killer-brought-to-justice-1.1275044) that it was in 2004 that new techniques matched a speck of DNA on Rachel's body to Napper, while other reports describe a positive match later, in 2007. We can't reconcile the dates with certainty, but all agree on the science and that he was charged in late 2007.

## The conviction and legal proceedings

The distinction between the two men matters, so here it is plainly:

| | Colin Stagg | Robert Napper |
|---|---|---|
| **Charged with Rachel's murder?** | Yes, in August 1993 | Yes, in late 2007 |
| **Tried?** | No. No jury was sworn in | No trial took place |
| **Outcome** | Judge excluded the evidence in September 1994; prosecution withdrew; he walked free | Pleaded guilty to manslaughter on the grounds of diminished responsibility on 18 December 2008 |
| **Convicted of murder?** | No | No. It was a manslaughter plea |
| **Sentence** | None | Ordered to be detained in Broadmoor indefinitely |
| **Later** | £706,000 compensation (2008); public police apology | Previously detained for the Bisset killings (1995) |

Napper's plea was entered at the Old Bailey before Mr Justice Griffith Williams. The court heard psychiatric evidence that he had a combination of paranoid schizophrenia and Asperger's syndrome. Diminished responsibility is a partial defence that reduces murder to manslaughter where a recognised mental disorder substantially impaired responsibility, which is why some coverage, including some about the Netflix documentary, describes him as "convicted" while the legal outcome was a manslaughter plea. Mental health diagnoses are not an explanation for violence on their own, and the court's concern was risk: the judge told Napper, "You are on any view a very dangerous man. You still present a very high risk of sexual homicide."

The judge said he hoped the family could now close a "long drawn-out chapter" of their lives. A detention order of this kind is not a prison sentence, and Broadmoor is a high-security psychiatric hospital; Netflix says Napper remains there.

## The impact on Rachel's family

Rachel's death changed the lives of her partner, her son and her parents, and press attention made it harder. André and Alex lived abroad for years. Rachel's parents told the court how their lives had been "trampled on by the media" and said that interview would be their last.

André complained to the IPCC in 2009. After the report in 2010 he said he believed the way to serve those who suffered most was to ensure that the lessons were learned. Alex, now an adult, says the police have never properly acknowledged their failures. Both have worked with Netflix on the documentary and the drama. In a statement to Netflix they said they hoped audiences would be left with "a testament to the tough battle of life we all face and to the power of faith, hope, love — and never giving up."

## What happened to the investigation over the years?

After Stagg walked free, police told Rachel's father they would keep the file open but would not pursue anyone else ([Crime + Investigation](https://crimeandinvestigation.co.uk/crime-files/rachel-nickell-case/trial)). It was the Bisset case and then the cold-case review that moved things forward. Napper was charged in late 2007 and pleaded guilty in December 2008, and the IPCC later examined what went wrong. Scotland Yard changed how it used specialist advisers and forensic science as a result, according to [Crime + Investigation](https://crimeandinvestigation.co.uk/crime-files/rachel-nickell-case/trial), though that is a general claim we can't independently measure.

## Netflix's The Murder of Rachel Nickell: what the documentary covers

[*The Murder of Rachel Nickell*](https://www.netflix.com/gb/title/81756346) is a one-off documentary film, 1 hour 36 minutes long, directed by Lucy Bowden, a BAFTA nominee known for *One Born Every Minute*. It was released on Netflix on 4 June 2026. On Netflix UK it is rated 15, and we checked on 25 November 2026 that its UK page is live.

According to Netflix, it combines archive footage, police interviews, forensic experts and first-hand accounts from André and Alex Hanscombe. Netflix lists these among the interviewees: André Hanscombe, Alex Hanscombe, Colin Stagg, Paul Britton, forensic scientist Dr Angela Gallop, Metropolitan Police officers and a Sky News reporter. Rachel's partner and son served as consultants.

It runs through the murder, the police inquiry, the wrongful focus on Stagg, the Bisset murders and the DNA breakthrough. Netflix's own explainer lays out the main arc: the sketch and profile, the undercover operation, the judge's ruling, the cold-case review and Napper's plea.

### The Witness, the companion drama

Netflix released *The Witness* on the same day. It is a three-part drama created by Rob Williams and based on Alex Hanscombe's memoir *Letting Go*. [Netflix describes it](https://www.netflix.com/tudum/articles/the-murder-of-rachel-nickell-release-date-news) as a fictionalised retelling of how André and Alex rebuilt their lives. The documentary is its true-crime counterpart.

## What makes this documentary different?

**It has the family's participation.** André and Alex are interviewed, and both consulted on the film. Alex, who was the two-year-old witness, appears as an adult speaking for himself.

**It centres the police failures rather than the killer.** Netflix's own pitch is that it examines "several investigative missteps", and [Rolling Stone's write-up](https://au.rollingstone.com/tv/tv-features/rachel-nickell-murder-netflix-documentary-96513/) quotes André saying a Crown Prosecution Service lawyer showed him a report that police negligence was partly to blame. [Time's feature](https://time.com/article/2026/06/01/rachel-nickell-true-story-the-witness-netflix/) is a useful companion on the true story behind both productions.

**It has Colin Stagg's own account.** After years of being told who he was, he speaks for himself. Rolling Stone quotes him: "I had low self-esteem before this all started. And this knocked me back further."

**It comes with a drama.** The pairing with *The Witness* is unusual, and the two work as different halves of the story.

## Is The Murder of Rachel Nickell worth watching?

<Callout type="verdict">
**Yes, if you want the family's perspective and a clear account of how the police failed.** We haven't watched the full film, so this verdict rests on Netflix's own material, the published interviews and reviews, and our reading of the case itself. **It will suit you if** you want an accessible way into one of Britain's most discussed miscarriages of justice, if you've seen *The Witness* and want the real events, or if you like documentaries that are built around the people affected.

**It may not suit you if** you want forensic or legal detail. At 96 minutes for a case spanning 16 years, it cannot cover what the podcasts below do. It also deals with a child witnessing his mother's murder and the killing of another child, so it is a hard watch.

Our advice: watch the documentary, then listen to the legal analysis from William Clegg KC (below), who defended Stagg, for what happened inside the courtroom.

## Similar British cases: miscarriages of justice and missed killers

The Rachel Nickell case has echoes in other British cases where the wrong person was pursued while the real offender went free. We describe only the main, well-documented points here.

- **Lynette White (Cardiff, 1988).** Three men were convicted in 1990 of her murder. The Court of Appeal quashed their convictions in December 1992 after finding the police had acted improperly. Jeffrey Gafoor was later convicted of the murder after a DNA breakthrough.
- **Lesley Molseed (Rochdale, 1975).** Stefan Kiszko was convicted in 1976 and his conviction was overturned in 1992. Ronald Castree was convicted of the murder in 2007 after a DNA match.
- **Jill Dando (London, 1999).** Barry George was convicted in 2001. His conviction was quashed in 2007 and he was found not guilty at a retrial in August 2008. The murder remains officially unsolved.

What links them with the Rachel Nickell case is how early certainty about a suspect hardened into tunnel vision, and how long it took for DNA technology and independent scrutiny to put it right. For more on how true crime coverage can affect people involved in cases like these, see our piece on [the ethics of true crime podcasting](/blog/ethics-of-true-crime-podcasting).

## The bottom line

Rachel Nickell was killed in 1992, and for 16 years the man responsible was out of reach, in part because of the decisions the police made. Colin Stagg was never convicted: his case collapsed in 1994, and he has been compensated and apologised to. Robert Napper, already detained for killing Samantha and Jazmine Bisset, admitted Rachel's manslaughter in December 2008. The IPCC concluded that police errors had cost Rachel, the Bissets and other women dearly.

Netflix's documentary gives André and Alex Hanscombe a voice and puts the police failures at the centre. To go further, listen to the lawyer who defended Stagg, then to a British narrative account of the whole case, using the order above.
