**Pamela Smart is the former New Hampshire school media coordinator convicted in 1991 of plotting the murder of her husband, Gregg Smart, who was shot dead by her 15-year-old lover, Billy Flynn, and his friends in May 1990.** Her trial was one of the first in the US to be televised from start to finish. She is serving life without parole, and in September 2026 a judge rejected her latest bid for a new trial. Netflix's documentary *A Deadly Affair: The Pamela Smart Story* is listed for 16 December 2026.

More than 35 years on, the Smart case is still argued about, still in the courts and still feeding films and podcasts. This guide covers what happened, what was proved at trial, what Smart says now, what we know about the Netflix film, and which podcasts are worth your time, with links.

Facts here come from reporting by the Associated Press, NHPR, the Concord Monitor, WMUR, InDepthNH and the 1991 *Portsmouth Herald*, checked on 25 November 2026. Where something is an allegation, a claim by one side or our own opinion, we say so.

<Callout type="verdict">
**The quick answer.** Pamela Smart was convicted by a jury, and every court since has upheld it. In 2024 she said publicly for the first time that she was responsible for Gregg's death. Her lawyers now argue that the trial was unfair, mainly because of transcripts of secret recordings that jurors were given. That is a fight about how she was convicted, not new evidence that someone else planned the killing. **Is the Netflix film worth watching?** We haven't seen it yet: it isn't out until 16 December. ## Who was Gregg Smart?

It's easy to lose Gregg Smart in this story, and many retellings do. He was 24 when he was killed, and he and Pamela had been married for less than a year. According to the *Portsmouth Herald*'s 1991 chronology of the case, the two met at a New Year's Eve party. He was killed in the home they shared in Derry, a town in southern New Hampshire.

His family have opposed Pamela's attempts to have her sentence shortened. When she accepted responsibility in 2024, Gregg's cousin Val Fryatt told WMUR that she had "just danced around it once again". Whatever you think of the later legal arguments, the fact at the centre of this case is that a young man was killed in his own hallway by people he had no reason to fear.

## Who is Pamela Smart?

Pamela Smart (née Wojas) was born in August 1967. In 1990, aged 22, she was the media services coordinator at Winnacunnet High School in Hampton. She is often described as a teacher, but it was a staff post rather than a teaching job.

Through a school programme called Project Self-Esteem, where she was an adult volunteer and he was a student volunteer, she met William "Billy" Flynn, a 15-year-old student. Flynn testified that in February 1990 she told him she loved him, and the two began a sexual relationship. He was a child and she was an adult working at his school. That is the first thing to hold on to when the story is told, as it often is, as a "forbidden romance".

## What happened: the murder of Gregg Smart

On the evening of 1 May 1990, Flynn and his friend Patrick "Pete" Randall, 17, went into the Smarts' condominium. According to the Associated Press's account of the case, they forced Gregg to his knees in the hallway; Randall held a knife to his throat and Flynn shot him in the head with a .38 revolver. Two more teenagers from Seabrook, Vance Lattime Jr and Raymond Fowler, waited in the car. The gun belonged to Lattime's father. The home was made to look as if Gregg had disturbed a burglary.

Pamela came home that night and found her husband dead. In the weeks that followed she gave interviews as a grieving widow. Meanwhile, the teenagers had talked. Tips from people in Seabrook led police to the gun and to the boys, and on 12 June 1990 Flynn, Randall and Lattime handed themselves in.

## The investigation and the tapes

To build a case against Pamela, investigators needed more than the word of teenagers facing murder charges. They got it from Cecelia Pierce, a teenage student who knew both Smart and Flynn and, it emerged, had heard about the plan before the killing.

Working with police, Pierce recorded two phone calls with Smart on 19 June 1990, then wore a wire to talk to her face to face in her school office on 12 and 13 July. On the tapes, Smart discussed what Pierce should say to investigators and spoke of fearing they would all end up in prison "for the rest of our lives". Those recordings became the core of the prosecution's case, and jurors later said they were what convinced them.

Pamela Smart was arrested at the school on 1 August 1990.

## The prosecution's case

Prosecutors Paul Maggiotto and Diane Nicolosi told the jury that Pamela Smart planned her husband's murder and used a teenager's devotion to get it done. Their main evidence was:

- **Billy Flynn's testimony.** Flynn told the court that Smart said she needed Gregg dead because she feared losing everything in a divorce, and that she threatened to end their relationship if he didn't do it.
- **The other teenagers.** Randall and Lattime, who had also pleaded guilty, backed up the account of a plan.
- **The Pierce tapes.** The recordings of Smart talking to Cecelia Pierce after the murder, which the prosecution said showed her guilty knowledge and her attempts to keep Pierce quiet. This is also the basis of the witness-tampering charge.

## The defence's case

Smart, represented by Mark Sisti and Paul Twomey, testified in her own defence and denied knowing about the plot. The defence argued that Flynn killed Gregg on his own, out of jealousy, and that the teenagers blamed her to get lighter sentences. She told the jury that on the Pierce tapes she had been playing along to find out what the teenagers knew.

The jury of seven women and five men did not believe her. On 22 March 1991 it found her guilty on all three charges. For being an accomplice to first-degree murder, New Hampshire law allowed only one sentence: life without parole. The teenagers who carried out the killing would all go free; the person convicted of planning it never would, and that gap still drives much of the debate.

## Why the Pamela Smart case became a national sensation

**It was on television.** The trial was shown from beginning to end on local TV and followed across the country. It's often described as the trial that showed TV networks there was an audience for live courtroom coverage, the format Court TV was built on.

**The story fitted a template.** A young, attractive school employee, a teenage lover, a murdered husband: the tabloids had their story before the jury heard any evidence. Smart was called "the most hated woman in America", a phrase Netflix's own synopsis repeats.

**It became fiction almost at once.** A TV film starring Helen Hunt aired in 1991, the year of the trial. Joyce Maynard's novel *To Die For* followed in 1992, and Gus Van Sant's 1995 film starring Nicole Kidman. For many people, Kidman's Suzanne Stone is still the "Pamela Smart" they know, which says a lot about how the real case became entertainment.

**It never ended.** She has spent 35 years seeking release, through appeals, commutation requests, interviews and, more recently, podcasts. Each new round reopens the argument.

## Where is Pamela Smart now?

Pamela Smart is 59 and has been held at Bedford Hills Correctional Facility for Women in New York since 1993. While in prison she has earned postgraduate degrees; her lawyer says she has three master's degrees and a doctorate.

### The commutation requests

Because her sentence is life without parole, Smart can't apply for parole. Her only route out besides the courts is to have her sentence commuted by New Hampshire's governor and Executive Council.

- **2022:** the Council unanimously refused to grant her a hearing, her third failed request.
- **2023:** the New Hampshire Supreme Court dismissed her attempt to challenge that refusal, saying it could not order the executive branch to reconsider.
- **2024:** in a letter to Governor Chris Sununu and a video for the Council, she accepted responsibility for the first time: "Today, I accept responsibility for the murder of my husband Gregg. I am the one to blame for his absence from this world." She said she had spent years deflecting blame "almost as if it was a coping mechanism."
- **May 2025:** Governor Kelly Ayotte said the case was "not deserving of a hearing" and declined to put it before the Council.

### The 2026 habeas corpus petition

In January 2026, her lawyers, led by attorney Matthew Zernhelt, filed a habeas corpus petition asking for a new trial or a new sentence. As the AP reported, their main argument is that jurors were given transcripts of the Pierce tapes that included words not audible on the recordings, so the transcripts steered what jurors heard. They also argued that media coverage tainted the jury, that the jury was given faulty instructions, and that her trial lawyers were ineffective.

The state replied that these issues had already been decided on appeal or could have been raised then. It pointed out that the trial judge told jurors to rely on what they heard on the tapes, not on the transcripts, and that the state Supreme Court found on appeal that the defence had not shown any particular inaccuracies that prejudiced her.

On 17 September 2026, Judge Kennedy of Merrimack County Superior Court sided with the state and dismissed the petition. His 55-page order said habeas corpus is "not a substitute for a direct appeal" and concluded that, even taking her facts as true, they could not show that inaccuracies in the transcripts had prejudiced her. ([NHPR](https://www.nhpr.org/nh-news/2026-09-18/pamela-smart-petition-dismissed-murder-trial-nh); [Concord Monitor](https://www.concordmonitor.com/2026/09/18/pamela-smart-conviction-upheld/))

The In Between Podcast, which has followed the petition closely, reported in early October that her lawyers had asked the court to reconsider. We have not seen a ruling on that reported in the mainstream press, and as of 25 November 2026 her conviction and sentence stand.

<Callout type="info">
**Facts and arguments.** A jury convicted Smart in 1991 and that conviction has been upheld on appeal. Her 2024 acceptance of responsibility was her own statement. The claims in her 2026 petition about transcripts, jury instructions and her trial lawyers are her lawyers' arguments; a judge has rejected them, though she may still be able to appeal.
</Callout>

## What happened to Billy Flynn and the others?

All four young men who took part in the killing pleaded guilty, testified against Smart and have been released. Flynn and Randall pleaded guilty to second-degree murder and were sentenced to 28 years to life; they were paroled in 2015. Lattime and Fowler, who waited in the car, were paroled in 2005.

Netflix's synopsis says Gregg Smart "was shot dead in the New Hampshire home he shared with his wife, Pamela", and that Pamela, "dubbed the most hated woman in America", became "the face of a trial that held the country transfixed". (The synopsis puts the killing in 1991; it was May 1990, and the trial was in 1991.)

Some pre-release coverage says the film is expected to include Smart's own account, alongside police archive, courtroom footage, news coverage and interviews. Netflix hasn't confirmed who appears. If she does speak, it will be her first major documentary interview since accepting responsibility in 2024, and one of the most interesting things about the film will be whether it asks her what changed.

### Is the Netflix documentary worth watching? Our verdict

We'll update this section once we've watched it. Until then, this is our honest take based on what Netflix has released and on the many retellings of this case we've already seen.

**Why it could be worth your time.** The courtroom footage is extraordinary in its own right, and a well-made documentary can show how a trial became a TV event in a way no podcast can. If the film gets Smart on camera after her 2024 admission, and presses her, that alone would justify it. A new generation also deserves a serious account of this case rather than the *To Die For* version.

**What would make it a weaker watch.** Netflix's own synopsis leads with "most hated woman in America". If the film repeats the 1991 tabloid framing, it will add little to what's already out there. Another risk is going the other way: retelling Smart's version of events without testing it against the trial evidence and the four men who say she planned the killing.

**What to watch for.** Does it treat Billy Flynn as what he was, a 15-year-old who was sexually exploited by an adult and who then committed a murder, rather than as a willing partner in a love story? Does Gregg's family get a voice? And does it explain the transcript arguments in her 2026 petition clearly, including why a judge rejected them?

### What the documentary may not cover in depth

A single film can't do everything. Based on how earlier treatments have handled the case, these are the areas most likely to be squeezed:

- **Gregg Smart as a person.** Most retellings give him a few minutes. The podcasts below that focus on him are the corrective.
- **The legal detail of the 2026 petition**, and the judge's reasons for dismissing it.
- **The four teenagers' lives since.** Their paroles, and what they have said since, are a story of their own.
- **The commutation politics** in New Hampshire, where governors have repeatedly refused to give her a hearing.
- **The media's role**, which *Captivated* (below) examines far more closely.

## The bottom line

Pamela Smart was convicted in 1991 of planning the murder of her husband, Gregg, by a teenager she had been sexually abusing, and the conviction has stood for 35 years. She accepted responsibility in 2024, and in 2026 a judge rejected her argument that her trial was unfair. Netflix's *A Deadly Affair*, out on 16 December, is the latest attempt to tell the story. Watch it, but start with Casefile, keep *Captivated* and Smart's own interviews in mind, and remember Gregg Smart, the 24-year-old at the centre of a story that has mostly been told about someone else.
