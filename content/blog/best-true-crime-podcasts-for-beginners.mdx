The true crime genre has produced some of the most listened-to podcasts on the planet. But if you're just starting out, walking into a landscape with thousands of shows is genuinely overwhelming. Which ones are actually worth your time? Which ones are too graphic to begin with? And what should you try after your first few?

This guide is built for people who are new to true crime podcasts — or who've tried one or two and want a clearer map of the territory. We've reviewed over 130 podcasts on this site, and we know exactly which ones are the best entry points.

## Why the Starting Point Matters

True crime podcasts vary enormously in tone, depth, and how they handle difficult material. Some are meticulously produced journalism — patient, careful, often brilliant. Others lean heavily on dramatisation, sensationalism, or graphic detail that can be jarring even for seasoned listeners.

Starting with the right show makes all the difference. Get it wrong and you might conclude that true crime isn't for you, when actually you just started in the wrong place.

The podcasts below are categorised by what they're best for, so you can match your starting point to what you actually want from the genre.

## The Best Starting Point: Serial

If you only try one, start here.

**Serial** — produced by Sarah Koenig and the team behind This American Life — invented the modern true crime podcast format when it launched in 2014. It's still among the best-produced, most rigorously reported shows the genre has ever produced.

Season 1 covers the 1999 murder of Hae Min Lee and the conviction of her ex-boyfriend Adnan Syed. It's a masterclass in investigative journalism delivered in audio form: patient, scrupulous, and genuinely uncertain about its own conclusions in a way that feels authentic rather than manipulative.

What makes it the ideal starting point: it treats listeners as intelligent adults, takes its time, never wallows in gore, and builds an obsessive narrative momentum without resorting to cheap cliffhangers.

## If You Want Weekly, Digestible Episodes: Crime Junkie

**Crime Junkie** is the most listened-to true crime podcast in the world, and for good reason. Host Ashley Flowers covers a new murder, disappearance, or cold case every week in tightly produced 30-minute episodes.

The format is immediately accessible: each episode opens with a brief summary of what you're about to hear, follows a clear chronological structure, and ends with advocacy for the victims. There's no graphic content, the research is solid, and Flowers has a genuine commitment to keeping cold cases in public consciousness.

It's not as deep or as journalistically ambitious as Serial — but as a weekly companion for your commute, it's almost unbeatable.

## If You Want Comedy and Camaraderie: My Favorite Murder

Some people come to true crime not for forensic detail but for the cathartic experience of processing dark stories with people who have a sense of humour about the whole enterprise. **My Favorite Murder** is built for that audience.

Hosts Karen Kilgariff and Georgia Hardstark cover true crime cases while constantly interrupting themselves, going on tangents, and acknowledging the absurdity of their own obsession. It's warm, funny, and surprisingly empathetic toward victims.

Fair warning: it's looser and less researched than the best investigative podcasts. But as an entry point for people who aren't sure they want to commit to something heavy, it's perfect.

## If You Want Cinematic, Produced Storytelling: Casefile

**Casefile** is an Australian true crime podcast with anonymous hosting — the host, known only as "the author," narrates each case with a calm, measured delivery that lets the stories speak for themselves.

The production quality is exceptional: music, pacing, and writing all work together to create something that feels cinematic. Cases range across Australia, the UK, and the US, and the research is consistently thorough.

For listeners who want excellent storytelling without comedy and without the personal-brand element that dominates many true crime podcasts, Casefile is the gold standard.

## If You Want Something That Solved a Real Crime: Your Own Backyard

**Your Own Backyard** by Chris Lambert covers the 1996 disappearance of Kristin Smart from Cal Poly San Luis Obispo. Lambert's investigation — conducted over years, and featuring interviews with witnesses who hadn't spoken publicly before — contributed directly to the 2021 arrest and 2022 conviction of Paul Flores.

This is what true crime podcasting can be at its best: patient, meticulous, and genuinely consequential.

## If You Want Wrongful Conviction Stories: Undisclosed or Serial Season 3

Wrongful conviction is one of the most compelling corners of the true crime genre, and it's where audio journalism has arguably done its most important work.

**Undisclosed** — hosted by attorney Rabia Chaudry and her team — applies serious legal analysis to cases they believe are wrongful convictions. It's demanding but rewarding.

Alternatively, **Serial Season 3** takes a different approach entirely: instead of a single case, Sarah Koenig spent a year inside a Cleveland courthouse, documenting the everyday reality of the American criminal justice system. It's a masterpiece of immersive journalism — and a perfect complement to the case-based true crime you'll encounter elsewhere.

## If You Want British True Crime: Casefile or Evil Has A Name

American true crime dominates the genre, but British cases have their own rhythms, institutions, and distinctive horrors. For British-focus true crime:

- **Casefile** covers UK cases regularly alongside Australian and American ones
- **The Lazarus Heist** (BBC World Service) is technically financial crime — North Korea's state-sponsored hacking — but it's some of the finest audio journalism produced anywhere

## What to Avoid as a Beginner

Three categories to steer clear of until you've found your footing:

**Graphic/exploitation podcasts.** Some shows lean heavily on disturbing details without editorial purpose. **Sword and Scale** is the most famous example — it uses raw 911 calls and court recordings to create an immersive but genuinely disturbing experience. Not wrong to exist, but a poor starting point.

**Heavily dramatised/scripted shows.** Some podcasts use actors to recreate crime scenes and conversations. The production quality varies wildly, and the genre is prone to sensationalism.

**Shows with weak research.** The true crime genre has a significant tail of podcasts that regurgitate Wikipedia summaries without adding anything. Life is short. Stick with the recommendations above until you've developed a feel for what good research looks like.

## The Natural Listening Journey

Most true crime listeners follow a similar path:

1. **Serial** or **Crime Junkie** as a gateway
2. Expanding into narrative storytelling (**Casefile**, **Dirty John**, **Teacher's Pet**)
3. Discovering investigative journalism (**Your Own Backyard**, **Dr. Death**, **The Coldest Case in Laramie**)
4. Developing specific interests: cold cases, wrongful conviction, financial crime, cults, specific geographies
5. Following the best creators across platforms rather than sticking to one show

The deeper you go, the more you'll discover that the genre is far richer than its reputation suggests.

## The Bottom Line

True crime podcasting is a remarkably broad genre — it contains everything from comedy chat shows to award-winning journalism. The key to getting started is matching your first few shows to what you actually want from it.

If you want to be gripped, **Serial** will grip you. If you want a weekly companion, **Crime Junkie** will never let you down. If you want something that changed the world, **Your Own Backyard** is proof that podcasting can matter.

Start with one. You'll know within two episodes whether you're hooked.
